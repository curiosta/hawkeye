Threats

External · harmful

Source playlist: Global Threats playlist (opens in a new tab)

A long-running measure of how close humanity is to global catastrophe is the Doomsday Clock, set each year by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists. In January 2026 it was set at 85 seconds to midnight, the closest it has ever been (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, 27 January 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Nuclear war

A widely shared shorter version (“two sworn enemies standing waist deep in gasoline, one with three matches, the other with five”) is a later paraphrase, not Sagan’s words.

  • About 12,200 warheads in nine states. The United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea and Israel together held an estimated 12,187 nuclear weapons in January 2026. Of these, 9,745 were in military stockpiles and about 4,012 were deployed with operational forces (SIPRI Yearbook 2026, data for January 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Two arsenals dominate, and stockpiles may soon grow. The United States and Russia hold almost 86% of all warheads. The world total is still falling only because both are dismantling retired warheads. SIPRI expects new warheads entering stockpiles to outpace dismantlement soon, and estimates that China’s arsenal grew from 600 to up to 620 warheads during 2025 (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Arms control has eroded. SIPRI reports that the strategic nuclear arms control framework has collapsed and the non-proliferation regime is under stress, while nuclear-armed states have become more secretive about their arsenals (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab), 2026).
  • More wars between states. Armed conflict took place in 49 states in 2025. The number of interstate conflicts doubled from three in 2024 to six in 2025, and four of the six involved a nuclear-armed state, including the fighting between India and Pakistan in May 2025 (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab), data for 2025).
  • Record military spending. World military expenditure rose for the 11th year in a row, to US$2,887 billion in 2025, or 2.5% of world GDP. This is the highest level SIPRI has recorded (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab), data for 2025).

Talk: I’ve studied nuclear war for 35 years -- you should be worried | Brian Toon | TEDxMileHigh (opens in a new tab) (November 2017)

Global warming

  • Human-caused and unequivocal. The IPCC concluded that human activities, mainly through greenhouse gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming. Global surface temperature was 1.1 °C above 1850–1900 levels in 2011–2020 (IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, 2023 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Eleven record-warm years in a row. 2015–2025 were the 11 warmest years on record, and 2025 was about 1.43 °C above the 1850–1900 average (WMO, State of the Global Climate 2025 (opens in a new tab)). The three years 2023–2025 averaged more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels, the first three-year period to do so (Copernicus, January 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Emissions are still rising. Fossil CO2 emissions were 37.8 billion tonnes in 2024 and were projected to rise by about 1% to 38.1 billion tonnes in 2025. Atmospheric CO2 was set to reach 425.6 ppm in 2025, 53% above the pre-industrial level (Global Carbon Budget 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • The 1.5 °C budget is almost used up. From the start of 2026, about 170 billion tonnes of CO2 could still be emitted for a 50% chance of keeping warming to 1.5 °C. That is around four years at 2025 emission levels (Global Carbon Budget 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Billions exposed. About 3.3 to 3.6 billion people live in places highly vulnerable to climate change (IPCC, 2023 (opens in a new tab)). In 2025, heatwaves, wildfires, drought, tropical cyclones, storms and floods caused thousands of deaths and billions in economic losses (WMO, data for 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Ocean warming and acidification

  • The ocean takes most of the heat. More than 91% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases is stored in the ocean. Ocean heat content reached a record high in 2025, and the ocean has warmed more than twice as fast in 2005–2025 as in 1960–2005 (WMO, State of the Global Climate 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Marine heatwaves almost everywhere. About 90% of the ocean surface had at least one marine heatwave in 2025 (WMO, data for 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • More acidic than in at least 26,000 years. The ocean absorbed about 29% of human CO2 emissions in 2015–2024, lowering its pH; present-day surface pH is unprecedented for at least 26,000 years (WMO, citing the IPCC (opens in a new tab)). In 2025 the ocean acidification planetary boundary was assessed as breached for the first time (PIK Planetary Health Check 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Sea level keeps rising. Global mean sea level in 2025 was around 11 cm higher than in 1993, and it has risen faster since 2012 than in 1993–2011 (WMO, data for 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Talk: Ocean acidification - the evil twin of climate change | Triona McGrath | TEDxFulbrightDublin (opens in a new tab) (2016)

Biodiversity loss

  • About a million species at risk. Around 1 million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, many within decades, more than ever before in human history (IPBES Global Assessment, 2019 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Wildlife populations down 73% on average. Monitored vertebrate populations fell by an average of 73% between 1970 and 2020, and freshwater populations by 85%. The index measures the average change in population size, not the number of animals lost (WWF/ZSL Living Planet Report 2024 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Five main drivers. In order of global impact, the main direct drivers of nature loss are changes in land and sea use, direct exploitation of organisms, climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species (IPBES, 2019 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Seven of nine planetary boundaries crossed. In 2025 scientists assessed seven of the nine Earth-system boundaries as breached, including biosphere integrity, land-system change and freshwater use. Only stratospheric ozone and aerosol loading remain within safe limits (PIK Planetary Health Check 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Depletion of natural resources

  • Extraction has tripled. Material resource use grew from 30 billion tonnes in 1970 to 106 billion tonnes. Without urgent action, extraction could rise by about 60% by 2060. Extracting and processing resources accounts for over 60% of planet-warming emissions and 40% of the health impacts of air pollution (UNEP Global Resources Outlook 2024 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Energy. Oil, natural gas and coal use all reached record highs in 2024, even as renewables set new deployment records for the 23rd year in a row. Around 730 million people still live without electricity (IEA World Energy Outlook 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Critical minerals. A single country is the dominant refiner for 19 of 20 energy-related strategic minerals, with an average market share of around 70%. As of November 2025, more than half of these minerals were subject to some form of export controls (IEA World Energy Outlook 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Coral reef. In the fourth global coral bleaching event, bleaching-level heat stress reached about 84.4% of the world’s coral reef area between January 2023 and September 2025, with mass bleaching in at least 83 countries and territories. The previous record was 68.2%, in 2014–2017 (NOAA Coral Reef Watch, June 2026 (opens in a new tab)). Almost a third of reef-forming coral species are threatened with extinction (IPBES, 2019 (opens in a new tab)).

Talk: There’s a global sand crisis and no one is talking about it | Vince Beiser | TEDxPenn (opens in a new tab) (2019)

Pandemics and drug resistance

  • COVID-19 cost about 22 million lives. Between 2020 and 2023, the pandemic was linked to an estimated 22.1 million excess deaths, including indirect deaths. That is more than three times the officially reported COVID-19 deaths, and it reversed a decade of gains in life expectancy (WHO, World health statistics 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Drug-resistant infections. Bacterial antimicrobial resistance was associated with more than 4.7 million deaths in 2021. About 1 in 6 laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide were resistant to antibiotics in 2023, and few new medicines are in development (WHO fact sheet (opens in a new tab)).
  • A pandemic agreement, adopted in 2025. The World Health Assembly adopted the WHO Pandemic Agreement on 20 May 2025. It opens for signature only after a separate annex on sharing pathogens and their benefits is adopted, and it enters into force after 60 ratifications (WHO, May 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Climate is shifting disease risk. Dengue is the world’s fastest-growing mosquito-borne disease. About half the world’s population is at risk, and reported cases are the highest ever recorded (WMO State of the Global Climate 2025, citing WHO (opens in a new tab)).

Talk: Maryn McKenna: What do we do when antibiotics don’t work any more? (opens in a new tab) (2015)

Economic shocks and debt

  • Steady but uneven growth. The IMF projects world growth of 3.0% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027. It notes that a war-related shock is weighing on energy importers and vulnerable economies, global disinflation has stalled, and downside risks from renewed conflict and financial-market repricing persist (IMF World Economic Outlook Update, July 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Interest bills crowding out development. Developing countries’ public debt reached $35 trillion in 2025, and their net interest payments reached $995 billion (UNCTAD, A World of Debt 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • 3.7 billion people affected. In 51 developing countries, home to 3.7 billion people, governments spend more on interest payments than on either health or education. That is 1.2 billion more people than in 2010 (UNCTAD, A World of Debt 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Forced displacement

  • One in 70 people. At the end of 2025, about 117.8 million people were forcibly displaced: refugees, asylum-seekers and people displaced inside their own country. UNHCR puts this at one in every 70 people, or 1.4% of the world’s population (UNHCR Global Trends, data for end-2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Most never leave their country. 68.7 million people were displaced inside their own country, and 41.6 million were refugees. Sudan remains the largest internal displacement crisis, with 9.1 million people displaced within the country (UNHCR Global Trends (opens in a new tab)).
  • A fall, but often through forced returns. Refugee numbers fell by 3% and internal displacement by 7% during 2025, mostly because of returns to Afghanistan, Syria, Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. UNHCR notes that many of these returns happened under adverse circumstances (UNHCR Global Trends (opens in a new tab)).
  • Neighbours carry the load. 65% of refugees are hosted in neighbouring countries, and low- and middle-income countries host 68% of refugees and others in need of international protection (UNHCR Global Trends, end-2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Hunger and food insecurity

  • 645 million people hungry. An estimated 645 million people, 7.8% of the world’s population, faced hunger in 2025 (range 608–696 million). This was the third year in a row of gradual progress, but the number is still above the level recorded in 2019, before the COVID-19 pandemic (SOFI 2026, PDF p. 17 (opens in a new tab), data for 2025).
  • Healthy diets out of reach. Almost 2.7 billion people were unable to afford a healthy diet in 2025 (SOFI 2026, PDF p. 53 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Short of the 2030 goal. Hunger is projected to keep falling, but between 510 and 520 million people may still be facing hunger in 2030, 56% of them in Africa (SOFI 2026, PDF p. 14 (opens in a new tab)).

Natural and cosmic hazards

  • 358 recorded disasters in 2025. The Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT) recorded 358 disasters related to natural hazards in 2025. They caused 16,607 deaths, affected 110.2 million people and caused US$169.68 billion in economic losses. Both the number of disasters and the number of deaths were below the 2005–2024 annual averages of 377 disasters and 57,429 deaths. The report notes that 2025 had no mega-disaster, that many heatwave deaths had not yet been counted, and that the 2025 figures may be revised (CRED, 2025 Disasters in Numbers, PDF pp. 2–4 (opens in a new tab), data for 2025).
  • About 16 major earthquakes a year. Based on records since about 1900, the US Geological Survey expects about 16 major earthquakes in any given year: 15 in the magnitude 7 range and one of magnitude 8.0 or greater. Yearly totals vary widely, from 23 in 2010 to 6 in 1989 (USGS, earthquake FAQ (opens in a new tab), long-term average).
  • Volcanic eruptions in 2025 below the recent average. The Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program counts 71 confirmed eruptions from 63 volcanoes during 2025, 29 of which began that year. Since 2000 the yearly averages are 78 eruptions and 36 new ones. The program states that volcanic activity has not been increasing (Smithsonian GVP, eruptions in 2025 (opens in a new tab), data to 31 March 2026).
  • Most large near-Earth asteroids found. More than 90% of near-Earth objects larger than one kilometre have been discovered. NASA’s Near-Earth Object Program is now working to find 90% of those larger than 140 metres (NASA CNEOS, Discovery Statistics (opens in a new tab), page checked October 2026).
  • Extreme solar storms. On NOAA’s space weather scale, a G5 (Extreme) geomagnetic storm (Kp = 9) occurs about 4 times per solar cycle. It can cause widespread voltage control problems, complete collapse or blackouts of some grid systems, damage to transformers and problems tracking satellites (NOAA SWPC, Space Weather Scales (opens in a new tab)). The G5 storm of 10–11 May 2024 was the largest geomagnetic storm in over 20 years (NOAA SWPC, May 2024 (opens in a new tab)).

Opportunities

External · helpful

Clean energy at record pace

  • 23 years of records. Renewables set new records for deployment in 2024 for the 23rd consecutive year (IEA World Energy Outlook 2025, executive summary (opens in a new tab)).
  • Electricity demand is rising fast. In the IEA’s main scenarios, electricity demand rises by around 40% to 2035, and by more than 50% in its Net Zero Emissions scenario (IEA WEO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Room to expand access. The IEA notes that countries such as India, Indonesia and China have shown how ambitious policies can widen access to modern energy, with around 730 million people still without electricity in 2025 (IEA WEO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

A defined path for emissions cuts

  • Emissions growth has slowed. Total human CO2 emissions grew 0.3% a year over 2015–2024, against 1.9% a year over 2005–2014 (Global Carbon Budget 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • The cuts are quantified. Pathways that limit warming to 1.5 °C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot cut greenhouse gas emissions by 43% [34–60%] by 2030 and 60% [49–77%] by 2035, from 2019 levels (IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, SPM Table SPM.1, 2023 (opens in a new tab)).
  • For 2 °C, the bar is lower. Pathways that limit warming to 2 °C (>67%) cut greenhouse gas emissions by 21% [1–42%] by 2030 and 35% [22–55%] by 2035 (IPCC AR6 SYR SPM, 2023 (opens in a new tab)).

New global agreements

  • A pandemic treaty. WHO member states adopted the world’s first Pandemic Agreement by consensus at the 78th World Health Assembly on 20 May 2025 (WHO, 20 May 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • The high seas. The BBNJ Agreement on marine biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction, adopted in June 2023, entered into force on 17 January 2026 (United Nations, BBNJ Agreement (opens in a new tab)).
  • 30 by 30 for nature. The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022) aims for at least 30% of land, inland water, coastal and marine areas to be effectively conserved by 2030, and for at least 30% of degraded ecosystems to be under effective restoration (CBD Decision 15/4, Targets 2 and 3 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Money for nature. The same framework aims to mobilise at least US$200 billion a year for biodiversity by 2030 (CBD Decision 15/4, Target 19 (opens in a new tab)).

Weaknesses

Internal · harmful

Off track on warming

  • Projected warming well above Paris goals. Full delivery of national pledges (NDCs) points to 2.3–2.5 °C of warming this century; current policies point to 2.8 °C (UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Overshoot of 1.5 °C now seen as inevitable. All IEA WEO-2025 scenarios exceed 1.5 °C of warming on a regular basis by around 2030 (IEA WEO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Oil, gas and coal still at records. In 2024, oil, natural gas and coal consumption all reached record highs (IEA WEO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Warning systems lag. As of 2023, only around half of countries provided heat early-warning services tailored to the health sector (WMO State of the Global Climate 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Basic needs still unmet

  • No electricity. Around 730 million people live without electricity, and nearly 2 billion rely on cooking methods harmful to health (IEA WEO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Healthy diets out of reach. About 2.7 billion people could not afford a healthy diet (SOFI 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Grids lag generation. Investment in power generation has risen almost 70% since 2015 to US$1 trillion a year, while grid spending has risen at less than half that pace, to US$400 billion (IEA WEO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Debt crowding out development

  • Interest above health or education. 51 developing countries spend more on interest than on either education or health. They are home to 3.7 billion people, 1.2 billion more than in 2010 (UNCTAD, A World of Debt 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • A trillion-dollar interest bill. Developing countries’ net interest payments on public debt reached US$995 billion in 2025. The median share of revenue spent on interest was 8.1%, nearly double the 2010 level (UNCTAD 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Higher rates for poorer countries. If developing countries borrowed at developed-country rates, they could save around US$500 billion a year (UNCTAD 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Ageing and low fertility

  • Below replacement. Two thirds of the world’s population live where average fertility is below 2.1 births per woman (UN WPP 2024 Summary of Results (opens in a new tab)).
  • More over-65s than children. By the late 2070s, people aged 65 and over are projected to reach 2.2 billion and outnumber children under 18 (UN WPP 2024 (opens in a new tab)).
  • More over-80s than infants. By the mid-2030s, people aged 80 or older are projected to reach 265 million, more than the number of infants (UN WPP 2024 (opens in a new tab)).

Land and sea heavily altered

  • Most land changed. Three quarters of the land-based environment and about 66% of the marine environment have been significantly altered by human actions (IPBES Global Assessment, 2019 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Food takes most land and water. More than a third of the world’s land surface and nearly 75% of freshwater resources are devoted to crop or livestock production (IPBES 2019 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Wetlands and fish stocks. Over 85% of wetlands present in 1700 had been lost by 2000. In 2015, 33% of marine fish stocks were harvested at unsustainable levels (IPBES 2019 (opens in a new tab)).

Weakening cooperation on security

  • Fewer peacekeepers. Personnel in multilateral peace operations fell by nearly 49% over the past decade, from 152,803 in December 2016 to 78,633 in December 2025 (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab)).
  • Arms control lapses. The 2010 US–Russia New START treaty expired in February 2026. SIPRI links growing nuclear secrecy partly to the degradation of arms control agreements with transparency measures, such as New START (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab)).
  • Health data gaps. As of end-2025, only 18% of countries reported mortality data to WHO within one year, and nearly one third had never reported cause-of-death data (WHO World Health Statistics 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Strengths

Internal · helpful

Cooperation that worked

  • The ozone layer. Of nine planetary boundaries, only stratospheric ozone and aerosol loading remain within safe limits. The ozone layer has largely recovered (PIK Planetary Health Check 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Policy can turn trends. PIK cites the Montreal Protocol and shipping rules as examples of international action that worked; global aerosol emissions are declining (PIK 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Conservation pays off. Conservation investment cut the extinction risk of mammals and birds by 29% on average in 109 countries (1996–2008) (IPBES 2019 (opens in a new tab)).

Health and living standards gains

  • Fewer HIV infections. New HIV infections fell by 40% between 2010 and 2024. The number of people needing treatment for neglected tropical diseases fell by 36% over the same period (WHO World Health Statistics 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Water, sanitation, cooking. Between 2015 and 2024, 961 million people gained safely managed drinking water, 1.2 billion sanitation, 1.6 billion basic hygiene and 1.4 billion clean cooking (WHO 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Hunger falling. The share of people facing hunger fell from 8.6% in 2022 to 8.1% in 2024 and 7.8% in 2025, the third consecutive year of decline (SOFI 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Nature’s stewards

  • Indigenous lands. Nature managed by Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities is generally declining less rapidly than in other lands (IPBES 2019 (opens in a new tab)).
  • A large share of land. Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities hold or manage about 28% of global land area, including over 40% of formally protected areas (IPBES 2019 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Forest loss slowing. The net rate of forest loss has halved since the 1990s (IPBES 2019 (opens in a new tab)).

The ocean buffer

  • Stores most excess heat. More than 91% of excess heat is stored in the ocean, a major buffer against higher temperatures on land (WMO State of the Global Climate 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Absorbs CO2. The ocean absorbed around 29% of the CO2 from human activities in 2015–2024 (WMO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • At a cost. The same absorption is lowering ocean pH, which harms marine life, fisheries and shellfish farming (WMO 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

A large, growing population

  • 8.2 billion people. The world population was about 8.23 billion on 1 July 2025 (UN WPP 2024 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Concentrated in Asia and Africa. Asia held 58.7% of people and Africa 18.8% in 2025 (UN WPP 2024 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Growth until the 2080s. The population is projected to peak at around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s (UN WPP 2024 Summary of Results (opens in a new tab)).
