Hawkeye analysis

Strategy cells on this page are Hawkeye’s own analysis. Each recommendation pairs a verified strength or weakness with a verified opportunity or threat, and compares it with a documented case from another place. The precedent facts are sourced. The pairing, the transfer and the recommendation are Hawkeye’s work, not official policy and not advice to any person or firm.

The documented responses listed lower on this page remain official programmes or instruments, each confirmed at their official source. Several of those responses already work in the same area as a recommendation. A recommendation is a pairing and a lesson from elsewhere. It is not a claim that Earth has no policy in that area. Every figure links to its source and gives the year it refers to.

The four cells

  • SO Responses that use strengths to pursue opportunities

    • Use treaties that already worked to keep new climate and health agreements on track

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Cooperation that worked · SWOT · Opportunities · New global agreements · SWOT · Opportunities · A defined path for emissions cuts

      Treat the Montreal Protocol as a working model for delivery of newer global agreements, including climate pledges and the 2025 Pandemic Agreement. Official UN processes already exist. This recommendation is a pairing of a documented success with those new instruments, not a substitute for them.

      Analogous case. A UN-backed scientific assessment of the Montreal Protocol, presented by WMO and UNEP, states that the phase out of nearly 99% of banned ozone-depleting substances has succeeded in safeguarding the ozone layer. If current policies remain in place, the ozone layer is expected to recover to 1980 values (before the appearance of the ozone hole) by around 2066 over the Antarctic, by 2045 over the Arctic and by 2040 for the rest of the world. The Antarctic ozone hole has been slowly improving in area and depth since the year 2000. (WMO/UNEP, 9 January 2023, updated 2 March 2023 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. The live strength is cooperation that already brought the ozone layer largely back within safe limits. The live opportunities are new agreements and a defined path for emissions cuts. Binding phase-outs, regular scientific assessment and nearly universal membership were central to the ozone result. The recovery dates are projections if current policies remain in place, not results already delivered. Greenhouse gases are far more embedded in energy, food and transport than the banned ozone chemicals were, so the same legal form will not automatically produce the same speed of results.

  • ST Responses that use strengths to reduce threats

    • Use the method that ended smallpox to prepare for the next pandemic threat

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Health and living standards gains · SWOT · Threats · Pandemics and drug resistance

      Use the method that ended smallpox, namely shared surveillance, vaccination where a safe tool exists, and coordinated national action, as the practical test of the new Pandemic Agreement. Official WHO processes already exist. This recommendation pairs live health gains with the live pandemic threat.

      Analogous case. On 8 May 1980, the 33rd World Health Assembly officially declared: The world and all its peoples have won freedom from smallpox (WHO, 8 May 2020 (opens in a new tab), quoting the Assembly). The same WHO release notes that a global commission certified eradication on 9 December 1979, and that the Assembly accepted that certification in 1980. It also records that The US$ 300m price-tag to eradicate smallpox saves the world well over US$ 1 billion every year since 1980.

      Why it transfers, and the limit. The world already has a documented case of a human disease removed by coordinated public action, and a new pandemic agreement adopted in 2025 (live SWOT). The main limit is that smallpox had a distinctive vaccine and no known animal reservoir. Many current threats, including new respiratory viruses and drug-resistant infections, do not share those features. COVID-19 also showed that solidarity can fail even when science is strong.

    • Apply ozone-treaty discipline to the climate threat

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Cooperation that worked · SWOT · Threats · Global warming

      Apply the Montreal Protocol’s mix of a binding phase-down, regular scientific assessment and almost universal membership to the harder work of cutting greenhouse gases. The Paris Agreement already exists. This recommendation is a lesson from a treaty that produced a measured recovery, not a claim that no climate treaty is in place.

      Analogous case. The same WMO/UNEP assessment confirms the ozone phase-out and the slow improvement of the Antarctic ozone hole since 2000 (WMO/UNEP, 9 January 2023 (opens in a new tab)). It also records an estimate for the later Kigali Amendment on hydrofluorocarbons, which are greenhouse gases, not ozone-depleting substances: the amendment is estimated to avoid 0.3–0.5°C of warming by 2100 (this does not include contributions from HFC-23 emissions).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Climate is already a live global threat, and ozone cooperation is a live strength. The Kigali figure is an estimate to 2100, not a temperature change already observed. The larger limit is scale and politics. Ozone chemicals were a narrower set of industrial gases. Fossil fuels and land use sit at the centre of today’s economies, and current national pledges still point well above the Paris temperature goals (live SWOT).

  • WO Responses that use opportunities to reduce weaknesses

    • Use the clean-energy build-out to close the electricity gap

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Basic needs still unmet · SWOT · Opportunities · Clean energy at record pace

      Steer part of the record global build-out of renewable power toward people who still lack electricity, including through grid extension, mini-grids and standalone solar. Official energy-access programmes already exist in many countries. This recommendation pairs the live access gap with the live clean-energy opportunity.

      Analogous case. Kenya’s official energy compact records a national result: Kenya has made significant strides in expanding electricity access, with national access levels rising from approximately 30% in 2014 to over 75% (65% on grid, 0.84% mini grid and 9.2% Standalone PV) as at 2024. This progress has largely been complemented by a strong focus on renewable energy development, which now accounts for 82% of the country’s total installed electricity generation capacity and 93% of actual energy consumption. (Kenya National Energy Compact 2025–2030 (opens in a new tab), figures as at 2024).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. About 730 million people still live without electricity (live SWOT), while renewable deployment keeps setting records. Kenya shows that access and a high renewable share can rise together. The same compact states the limit: Approximately 25% of Kenyans still do not have access to electricity, with the rural areas disproportionately low on access. Over 69% of households rely on traditional biomass for cooking. A national success in one country does not close a global gap of hundreds of millions, and clean cooking can lag even where electricity access rises.

    • Close the emissions gap with policies that already cut pollution while economies grew

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Off track on warming · SWOT · Opportunities · A defined path for emissions cuts · SWOT · Opportunities · Clean energy at record pace

      Use policy mixes that already cut greenhouse-gas emissions while economies grew, as a practical way to close the gap between current policies and the Paris temperature goals. The Paris Agreement and national pledges already exist. This recommendation is a lesson from a measured regional cut, not a new global treaty.

      Analogous case. The European Environment Agency, in its 9 November 2026 press release on the Trends and Projections report, states: In 2025, net EU greenhouse gas emissions under the scope of the European Climate Law dropped by 39% below 1990 levels, and This drop also came while gross domestic product and populations grew by 74% and 8% respectively over the same period. The figures for 2025 are preliminary. The same release records that the pace of cuts slowed in 2025, and that year-on-year emissions rose in domestic transport and buildings (EEA, 9 November 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Current policies still point to about 2.8 °C of warming this century (live SWOT). The EU case shows that deep cuts and economic growth can run together over three decades. The 2025 figures are preliminary, and the EU started from a different industrial and institutional base, including a carbon market. Transport and buildings in the EU still saw emissions rise in 2025. The same path will be harder where coal use and energy demand are still rising fast.

  • WT Responses that reduce weaknesses and limit threats

    • Renew coordinated debt relief so interest costs do not crowd out basic services

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Debt crowding out development · SWOT · Threats · Economic shocks and debt

      Renew coordinated debt relief for countries where interest costs now exceed spending on health or education, so that a new shock does not deepen that squeeze. Official international financial institutions already work on debt. This recommendation pairs the live debt weakness with the live shock-and-debt threat.

      Analogous case. The World Bank’s HIPC brief states: The HIPC and related Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (MDRI) programs have relieved 37 participating countries of more than $100 billion in debt. To date, 37 countries — 31 of them in Africa — have debt-relief for which they were eligible through the HIPC Initiative and the MDRI. The same brief states the limit in official words: But challenges remain to ensure that debt burdens do not return to unsustainable levels. (World Bank, HIPC brief, 9 May 2024 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Fifty-one developing countries now spend more on interest than on education or health, and they are home to 3.7 billion people (live SWOT). Past coordinated relief did reduce a large stock of debt. The World Bank itself warns that unsustainable burdens can return. Today’s creditors, currencies and bond markets also differ from the HIPC period. Relief alone will not close the gap if new borrowing rises faster than revenues.

    • Pay landholders to restore forests and ease pressure on land and species

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Land and sea heavily altered · SWOT · Threats · Biodiversity loss

      Pay landholders for verified forest protection and restoration, so that pressure on land and species can ease where conversion is still rapid. Official biodiversity targets already exist. This recommendation pairs the live land-use weakness with the live extinction threat, and uses a documented national reversal of forest loss.

      Analogous case. The World Bank records Costa Rica’s result: It is the first tropical country in the world to have reversed deforestation. Its highly biodiverse tropical rainforests now cover close to 60% of the country, having shrunk as low as 40% in 1987. Costa Rica also received a first Forest Carbon Partnership Facility payment of $16.4 million for independently verified emission reductions, for reducing 3.28 million tons of carbon emissions in 2018 and 2019 (World Bank, 16 November 2022 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Three quarters of the land-based environment, and about 66% of the marine environment, are already significantly altered, and around a million species are at risk (live SWOT). Paying for verified results can change land-use incentives. The analogy is limited because Costa Rica is a small country with a long-running payment scheme and strong protected-area governance. The same tools will be harder in large, transboundary forest regions and in places where land rights are weak or contested.

Documented global responses 6

Listed in no particular order. None is ranked.

  • The Paris Agreement

    Adopted by Parties to the UNFCCC (in force since 4 November 2016)

    Its aim is Holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels (Paris Agreement (opens in a new tab), Article 2.1(a)).

    Related SWOT · Threats · Global warming · SWOT · Weaknesses · Off track on warming · SWOT · Opportunities · A defined path for emissions cuts

  • The COP28 energy goals

    Adopted by Parties to the Paris Agreement, decision 1/CMA.5 (December 2023)

    The goals are tripling renewable capacity and doubling the rate of efficiency improvement by 2030. IRENA’s third tracking report says capacity reached 5.15 TW at the end of 2025 against a target of 11.2 TW (IRENA, 21 September 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

    Related SWOT · Opportunities · Clean energy at record pace

  • Early Warnings for All

    Adopted by United Nations, led by WMO and UNDRR

    119 countries reported a multi-hazard early warning system in the 2025 status report, against a goal of everyone by the end of 2027 (WMO and UNDRR, 2025 report (opens in a new tab)).

    Related SWOT · Threats · Natural and cosmic hazards · SWOT · Threats · Global warming

  • The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework

    Adopted by Parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity, decision 15/4 (December 2022)

    It has 23 action-oriented global targets for 2030 (Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (opens in a new tab)), among them 30% of land and sea conserved.

    Related SWOT · Threats · Biodiversity loss · SWOT · Weaknesses · Land and sea heavily altered · SWOT · Strengths · Nature’s stewards

  • The BBNJ Agreement on the high seas

    Adopted by United Nations member states (in force since 17 January 2026)

    It entered into force on 17 January 2026 and its first Conference of the Parties is due in January 2027 (United Nations, BBNJ Agreement (opens in a new tab)).

    Related SWOT · Threats · Ocean warming and acidification · SWOT · Strengths · The ocean buffer

  • The WHO Pandemic Agreement

    Adopted by World Health Assembly (adopted 20 May 2025)

    Its annex on sharing pathogens and benefits is still being negotiated, and the agreement opens for signature only once that annex is adopted (WHO, 19 May 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

    Related SWOT · Threats · Pandemics and drug resistance

How this TOWS matrix works

For Earth, each cell will list responses at global scale that a named authoritative body (for example a UN agency, the IPCC or the parties to a treaty) has proposed, adopted or assessed.

Documented responses are attributed to the body behind them and linked to their official text or assessment. Those responses are not ranked. Strategy cells, where filled, are Hawkeye’s own analysis: each recommendation pairs a verified strength or weakness with a verified opportunity or threat, and compares it with a documented case from another place. They are not official policy and not advice. The official-response list is completed after all four SWOT quadrants are written.
