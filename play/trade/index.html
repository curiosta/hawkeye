Trade or tariff

Two countries sell goods to each other every year. Each year, each one decides: keep its market open, or put up a tariff (a tax on goods coming in from the other country). What happens depends on whether they expect to meet again.

What you’ll learn

  • What a tariff is, and why putting one up can look tempting in a single year.
  • Why the same choice can work out differently when two sides deal with each other again and again.
  • How five simple ways of playing (strategies) score against each other.
  • What researchers found about cooperation based on returning the favour (reciprocity).

Before you play

The situation, in everyday words

Picture two farmers in neighbouring villages. One grows rice, the other grows vegetables, and every week they sell to each other. While both let the other sell freely, both have more choice of food and more buyers for their own crop. Now suppose one farmer starts charging the other a fee at the village gate. For a while that farmer collects the fee and still sells freely next door. But the neighbour may start charging a fee too, and then both are worse off than when trade was free.

This chapter turns that story into a game between two countries. Each year a country can keep its market (the place where goods are bought and sold; here, the whole country) open to the other country’s goods, or put up a tariff.

The question is simple: when does staying open work out, and when does a tariff pay? In this game the answer turns on one thing: whether the two sides expect to meet again.

Words you’ll meet

Trade
Buying and selling goods or services. Between countries, goods sold abroad are exports and goods bought from abroad are imports.
Tariff
A tax a country charges on goods brought in from another country. It makes those goods dearer to buy at home.
Open market
Here, a country that lets the other country’s goods in without a tariff.
Round
One year of the game. Both countries choose at the same time, without knowing the other’s choice.
Points
A made-up score for how well a country did in a round. More points means better off in the game.
Strategy
A fixed plan for how to play, such as “always stay open” or “copy what the other side did last time”.
Repeated game
A game the same two players play many times, so each can remember and react to what the other did before.
Reciprocity
Treating others the way they treated you: openness for openness, a tariff for a tariff.
Prisoner’s dilemma
The name economists give to this pattern of points: in one round each side gains by acting alone, but both do worse if both act that way. The name is only a label; no prison is involved here.
Tournament
Here, a round-robin: every strategy plays every other strategy, and the points are added up.

The rules

Each round, both countries choose at the same time. Neither can see the other’s choice until both have chosen. The points are made up. Here is every possible result:

Points for each country in one round (you first, then them)
Your choiceThey keep openThey put up a tariff
You keep openYou 3, they 3You 0, they 5
You put up a tariffYou 5, they 0You 1, they 1

How to read it. Both open: 3 each, because trade flows both ways. Both tariff: 1 each, because trade shrinks on both sides. One tariffs while the other stays open: the one with the tariff gets 5 and the open one gets 0.

Why it’s a puzzle. Take the other side’s choice as fixed and compare your two options. If they keep open, a tariff gets you 5 instead of 3. If they put up a tariff, a tariff gets you 1 instead of 0. So in any single round, a tariff scores more for you whatever they do. But if both sides reason that way, both get 1 point instead of the 3 they could have had by both staying open. This is the payoff pattern of the prisoner’s dilemma.

How this page works

  1. Play six rounds. You are one country. The other country follows a fixed strategy you can’t see. After each round the page tells you what both sides chose, the points, and why.
  2. Watch a tournament. Five strategies play each other. A slider sets how many rounds each pair plays, from 1 to 20.
  3. Read what it means. Below the game: the main lesson in plain words, what it means in real life, and what the game leaves out.

What the model typically shows

How to read the charts. Each bar is one strategy’s total points after it has played every other strategy. A longer bar means more points. With 1 round per match, Hardliner comes first with 20 points. With 10 rounds per match, Mirror and Grudge-holder share first with 99 points, and Hardliner is placed 4th with 96. In a one-off match, the tariff pays; when the same pairs meet many times, strategies that start open and answer tariffs with tariffs do best.

After you play

Takeaway

In a single round of this game, putting up a tariff scores more whatever the other side does. When the same two sides expect to meet many times, strategies based on reciprocity can start cooperating, do well against a wide range of other strategies, and resist being displaced once they are common.

In plain words: If two countries meet only once, putting up a tariff gets you more points whatever the other side does. If the same two expect to meet again and again, strategies that answer openness with openness and a tariff with a tariff can start cooperating, do well against many different kinds of partner, and are hard to push out once many players use them.

What this means in real life

The game is a very simple picture of trade, but it shows one idea clearly. A move that pays off once can cost more later, when the other side remembers it and answers back. In the model, when the same two sides expect to meet many times, strategies that return openness for openness and a tariff for a tariff end up keeping their markets open with each other.

Real countries do more than choose “open” or “tariff”. They also agree on shared rules, and that kind of cooperation can grow or weaken over time. Two examples already on Hawkeye’s Earth page:

What this model leaves out

  • Real trade involves many goods, many countries and politics at home, not one yes-or-no choice each year.
  • Real countries can talk, sign agreements and take disputes to panels that settle them. Here they only see each other’s last move.
  • The points are made up and the same for both sides.
