The shared pasture

Four herders share one village pasture. The grass grows back, but only so fast. Each season, each herder decides how much their animals eat: a modest share or a large one. How long does the grass last?

What you’ll learn

  • What a “commons” is: something many people use, where what one takes leaves less for the others.
  • Why taking a bit more can make sense to each person but run the shared thing down for everyone.
  • How fast the grass grows back, and why that speed matters.
  • What field studies found about groups that make and enforce their own rules.

Before you play

The situation, in everyday words

Imagine a village with one big field of grass that everyone may use. Four families graze goats on it. Each family wants its goats well fed, because more grass eaten means more milk to drink and sell. If one family lets its goats eat a little extra, that family gets all the benefit, while the cost (a little less grass) is shared by all four. So each family, thinking only of itself, is tempted to take more. If every family does the same, the field can be eaten bare, and then no one has anything.

Grass grows back, but only so fast. That is the heart of this chapter: a shared resource that regrows slowly, and several people each deciding how much to take.

Fishing grounds, water under the ground and forests can work in a similar way. Economists call such resources common-pool resources, or commons for short.

Words you’ll meet

Commons
Short for common-pool resource: something many people can use, where what one person takes leaves less for others, such as a shared pasture, a fishing ground or groundwater.
Herder
Someone who looks after grazing animals such as goats, sheep or cows.
Stock
How much of the resource there is right now. Here, the units of grass left in the pasture.
Unit
A made-up amount of grass. The pasture holds at most 100 units.
Share
How much grass one herder’s animals eat in a season: modest (1.5 units) or large (3 units).
Season
One turn of the game: everyone grazes, then the grass grows back.
Regrowth
The grass that grows back after grazing, before the next season.
Logistic growth
A pattern where something grows slowly when there is very little of it, fastest when it is about half full, and slowly again as it nears its limit.
Rules, monitoring and enforcement
Agreeing how much each person may take, checking whether people keep to it, and applying penalties when they don’t.

The rules

The pasture starts full, with 100 units of grass, and can never hold more than 100. Each season has two steps:

  1. Grazing. Each of the four herders takes either a modest share (1.5 units) or a large share (3 units). If there isn’t enough grass for everyone, the herders get what is left.
  2. Regrowth. The grass that is left grows back a little. It regrows fastest when the pasture is about half full (logistic growth). When the pasture is nearly bare it regrows by up to 25% of what is left, which is very little grass. When it is nearly full, there is little room left to grow.

The limit. At its fastest, the pasture grows back a little over 6 units a season. Together the four herders can take at most about 6 units a season without running the pasture down. Four modest shares add up to exactly 6 units (4 × 1.5). Each herder who switches to a large share adds 1.5 units more, which pushes the total above what the pasture can grow back, even at its fastest.

All numbers are made up.

How this page works

  1. Herd for ten seasons. You are one of the four herders and you choose your share. The other three choose theirs at random, like tossing a coin. After each season the page tells you what everyone took, how much grass grew back and how much is left.
  2. Run thirty seasons. Choose how many of the four herders take the modest share, then watch the grass over thirty seasons.
  3. Read what it means. Below the game: the main lesson in plain words, what it means in real life, and what the game leaves out.

What the model typically shows

How to read the chart. Each line follows the grass left in the pasture, season by season, for a different number of herders taking the modest share. With 4 of 4 on the modest share, 69 units are left after 30 seasons; with 2 of 4 on the modest share, the grass runs out in season 24; with 0 of 4 on the modest share, the grass runs out in season 14. The more herders take the large share, the sooner the pasture is eaten bare.

After you play

Takeaway

In this model, when each herder weighs only their own gain from taking more, a shared stock that regrows slowly tends to be run down. Field studies find that groups who make, monitor and enforce their own rules often keep such resources in use for long periods, though not in every case.

In plain words: In this game, if every herder thinks only about their own extra grass, a pasture that grows back slowly tends to get eaten down. In real life, researchers who studied groups sharing such resources found that groups who make their own rules, check whether people keep them and enforce them often keep the resource going for a long time, though not in every case.

What this means in real life

The game shows a trap that is easy to fall into. Each herder gets the full benefit of taking more, but the cost is shared by everyone. So taking more can look sensible to each person while the pasture runs down for all. In the model this happens when each herder weighs only their own gain; the game has no rules at all, only choices. The idea is often called “the tragedy of the commons”, after the title of Garrett Hardin’s 1968 article.

Real communities are not stuck with that result. Field studies, such as those in Elinor Ostrom’s 1990 book, found that groups who make, monitor and enforce their own rules often keep shared resources in use for long periods, though not in every case.

What this model leaves out

  • Real pastures, fishing grounds and groundwater have weather, prices and many more users.
  • Here the only “rule” is how many herders keep to the modest share. The game does not show how rules are made, checked or enforced.
  • Ownership by a government or by individuals is not modelled. The Nobel summary notes that each can do better or worse than management by the users themselves, depending on the case.
