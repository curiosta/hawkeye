Interest and growth

A government owes money equal to 60% of everything its country produces in a year. Every year interest is due on that debt, and every year the economy grows by some amount. Does the debt get bigger or smaller compared with the economy?

What you’ll learn

  • What GDP is, and what it means to measure debt as a share of it (the debt-to-GDP ratio).
  • How interest makes debt grow, and how a growing economy makes the same debt a smaller share.
  • What a primary balance is, and the difference between a surplus and a deficit.
  • Why the gap between the interest rate and the growth rate matters so much in the standard formula.

Before you play

The situation, in everyday words

Start with a family (all numbers made up, in any currency). The family earns 100,000 a year and owes 60,000 on a loan, so it owes 60% of a year’s income. The lender charges interest of 4% a year, so the debt grows by 2,400 in a year if nothing is repaid. If the family’s income rises by 2% in that year, to 102,000, the debt (now 62,400) has grown faster than the income. It is now a bigger share of income: about 61%. If instead income had risen faster than the interest rate, say by 5% to 105,000, the same 62,400 would be a smaller share, about 59%, even with nothing repaid.

The family has one more lever: paying back part of the loan out of its income makes the debt smaller.

The game below uses the same arithmetic for a government, with new names. The government’s “income” is the size of the whole economy, called GDP. The interest rate is r and the growth rate of the economy is g. What the government’s own budget adds to or pays off the debt each year, leaving interest aside, is the primary balance. A household is only a rough comparison to make the arithmetic easier to picture. Governments differ from families in many ways, and the game is about governments.

Words you’ll meet

Debt
Money owed. Government debt is what a government has borrowed and not yet paid back.
GDP
Gross domestic product: the total value of all the goods and services produced in a country in a year. Think of it as the size of the economy.
Debt-to-GDP ratio
Debt divided by GDP, written as a percentage (the “debt ratio”). A ratio of 60% means the debt equals 60% of one year’s GDP.
Interest rate (r)
What a borrower pays each year for the use of the money, as a percentage of the debt.
Real
With inflation (the general rise in prices) taken out. A real interest rate of 3% means 3% above the rise in prices.
Growth rate (g)
How much bigger the economy gets in a year, in real terms, as a percentage.
Primary balance
The government’s revenue (mostly taxes) minus its spending, leaving out interest payments, as a share of GDP.
Primary surplus
A positive primary balance: revenue is more than spending before interest, and the extra can go towards the interest or the debt.
Primary deficit
A negative primary balance: spending before interest is more than revenue, so the government borrows more.
Percentage point
The plain difference between two percentages. Going from 60% to 62% is a rise of 2 percentage points.

The rules

The game starts with debt at 60% of GDP. Each year, three things move the debt ratio:

  1. Interest pushes it up. Interest is added to the debt at rate r.
  2. Growth pulls it down. The economy grows at rate g, so the same debt is a smaller share of a bigger economy.
  3. The primary balance. A surplus is taken off the ratio; a deficit is added to it.

Put together, each year the debt ratio changes by (r − g) / (1 + g) × last year’s ratio, minus the primary balance (tax revenue minus spending before interest, as a share of GDP). Here r is the real interest rate and g is real growth.

A worked example. Debt is 60% of GDP, r is 4%, g is 2% and the primary balance is zero. The gap between them is 4 − 2 = 2. Dividing by 1.02 (one plus growth) and multiplying by 60 gives about 1.2. So the ratio rises from 60% to about 61.2% in one year. A primary surplus of about 1.2% of GDP would have held it at 60%.

The key idea. If r is bigger than g, the ratio rises unless there is a big enough surplus. If r is smaller than g, the ratio falls unless there is a big enough deficit. The starting numbers are made up.

What the model typically shows

How to read the chart. Each line follows the debt ratio over 30 years, starting at 60% of GDP, with the primary balance at zero. When r is 4% and g is 2%, the ratio rises to 107.4%; when r and g are both 2%, the ratio stays at 60%; when r is 1% and g is 3%, the ratio falls to 33.3%. With no surplus or deficit, interest above growth pushes the ratio up and interest below growth pulls it down.

After you play

Takeaway

In the standard debt-dynamics identity, the change in the debt ratio depends on the gap between the interest rate and the growth rate, and on the primary balance. When the interest rate is above the growth rate, a primary surplus is needed just to hold the ratio steady. When it is below, the ratio can stay level or fall even with a primary deficit, depending on the deficit’s size.

In plain words: Each year, what happens to the debt ratio depends on two things: how the interest rate compares with the growth rate, and whether the government’s budget, leaving out interest, is in surplus or deficit. If interest is higher than growth, the government has to take in more than it spends before interest just to stop the ratio rising. If interest is lower than growth, the ratio can stay level or even fall while the government spends a little more than it takes in, depending on how big that gap is.

What this means in real life

The game shows why the formula puts two numbers side by side: the interest rate a government pays and the growth rate of its economy. When interest is higher than growth, the debt ratio rises unless the government runs a big enough primary surplus. When interest is lower than growth, the ratio can stay level or fall even with a primary deficit, depending on the deficit’s size.

  • In 51 developing countries, home to 3.7 billion people, governments spend more on interest than on either health or education (UNCTAD, A World of Debt 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Developing countries’ net interest payments on public debt (interest paid minus interest received) reached US$995 billion in 2025. If they borrowed at developed-country rates, they could save around US$500 billion a year (UNCTAD 2026 (opens in a new tab)). See Earth: Debt crowding out development.

What this model leaves out

  • Here interest rates do not change when debt rises. In reality lenders may ask for more interest as debt rises (Blanchard discusses this risk).
  • Inflation, exchange rates (the price of one currency in another) and debt owed in foreign currency are left out.
  • Here growth does not depend on what the government spends or taxes.
