Antarctica has no permanent population, no national government and no national economy, so population, GDP, human development and national emissions figures are not shown. Military measures and nuclear explosions are prohibited by the Antarctic Treaty (Articles I and V). Station figures are as reported by national programmes to COMNAP. A land-only area from an official survey was not found and is not shown. For 2025–26, IAATO’s own page gives 85,195 landed visits, 27,217 cruise-only visitors and 1,106 deep-field visitors (IAATO, Data & Statistics (opens in a new tab)), counted separately and not added up here.

T

Threats

External · harmful

Ice loss and record-low sea ice

  • About 150 billion tonnes of ice lost a year. Research based on satellite data indicates that between 2002 and 2023, Antarctica shed an average of 150 billion metric tons of ice per year, adding to global sea level rise. (NASA, Antarctic Ice Mass Loss 2002–2023 (opens in a new tab), GRACE and GRACE-FO satellite data)
  • Loss is concentrated in West Antarctica. Areas in East Antarctica experienced modest amounts of mass gain due to increased snow accumulation. However, this gain is more than offset by significant ice mass loss on the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (dark red) over the 21-year period. (NASA, 2023 (opens in a new tab))
  • What the ice sheet holds. We calculate a sea level equivalent (SLE) of 57.9 ± 0.9 m for the Antarctic Ice Sheet (Supplementary Table 3), which is close to the Bedmap2 estimate of 58.3 m. (Morlighem et al., BedMachine Antarctica, Nature Geoscience, 2020 (opens in a new tab))
  • Record-low summer sea ice in 2023. Antarctic sea ice has likely reached its minimum extent for the year, at 1.79 million square kilometers (691,000 square miles) on February 21, 2023, according to scientists at the National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC) at the University of Colorado Boulder. The 2023 minimum is the lowest in the 45-year satellite record. NSIDC adds: NSIDC scientists stress that the Antarctic sea ice extent number is preliminary (NSIDC, 27 February 2023 (opens in a new tab))
  • The 2026 minimum was near average. NSIDC gives a preliminary minimum of 2.58 million km² on 26 February 2026: The 2026 minimum ranks sixteenth lowest in the 48-year satellite record. It was 730,000 km² above the 2023 record low (NSIDC, 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Krill fishing pressure

  • A record catch in 2025. In 2025 a total 624918 tonnes of E. superba was caught from Subareas 48.1, 48.2 and 48.3 (Tables 3–6). (CCAMLR Fishery Report 2025, Antarctic krill in Area 48 (opens in a new tab), section 2.1; the 2025 season ran from 1 December 2024 to 30 November 2025)
  • Above the trigger level. The Scientific Committee noted record krill catches in Statistical Area 48 during the 2024/25 season, which exceeded the trigger level of 620 000 tonnes specified in CM 51-01 by 4 917 tonnes. More than 50 % of this catch was taken from Subarea 48.1, corresponding to a doubling of the CM 51-07 (2023) limit for that Subarea (WG-FSA-2025, paragraph 2.5). (SC-CAMLR-44 report (opens in a new tab), paragraph 2.13)
  • The fishery closed early. Catches were taken faster than in any previous season with the result that the trigger level was reached and the fishery was closed on 1 August 2025. (SC-CAMLR-44 report (opens in a new tab), paragraph 2.18). CCAMLR’s 2025 fishery report describes it as the first time the fishery was closed (it prints the date as 1 August 2026, which does not match the season it covers).
  • The previous record was a year earlier. In 2024 a total 498350 tonnes of E. superba was caught from Subareas 48.1, 48.2 and 48.3 (Tables 3–6). and The catch in Area 48 reached its historical maximum in 2024. (CCAMLR Fishery Report 2024 (opens in a new tab), sections 2.1 and 1.1)

The rules on where krill may be caught are listed under Weaknesses.

Non-native species and ecosystem change

  • First on the environment committee’s climate list. The Committee for Environmental Protection’s Climate Change Response Work Programme lists, as climate-related issue 1: Enhanced potential for non-native species (NNS) introduction establishment (ATCM XLVI Final Report, Appendix 2 (opens in a new tab))
  • Gaps the committee lists. Among the needs listed for that issue: Framework for surveillance for non-native species establishments in marine, terrestrial and freshwater environment and Response strategy for suspected NNS introductions (CCRWP (opens in a new tab))
  • Ocean acidification. The same work programme lists issue 4: Ecosystem change due to ocean acidification (CCRWP (opens in a new tab)). It gives no figure.
  • Visitor numbers (industry data). The total number of visitors (Vessel and Deep Field) in the 2024-25 season was 118,491. (IAATO report to ATCM 47 (opens in a new tab)). The committee’s work programme does not give a visitor figure, and the two sources are shown side by side here without a link between them being claimed.

O

Opportunities

External · helpful

Polar science: the next International Polar Year and the oldest ice

  • A Fifth International Polar Year, 2032–2033. Norway is taking on a leading role in global polar research as UiT The Arctic University of Norway, together with the Norwegian Polar Institute and partners from South Korea and Chile, will host the secretariat for the Fifth International Polar Year (IPY-5). (WMO, news from Members, 12 August 2026 (opens in a new tab), item from the Norwegian Meteorological Institute)
  • Ten years of coordination, with a climate focus. Tromsø will serve as the main hub for coordinating global polar research over a ten-year period leading up to, during, and following the official Polar Year in 2032–2033. The International Polar Year aims to address urgent global challenges by advancing polar research, with a particular focus on the impacts of climate change in the Arctic and Antarctic. (WMO, 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • An ice core reaching back 1.5 million years. This project will recover the first ever ice core record reaching beyond 1 million years (1 Myr) ago, extending our knowledge on climate and greenhouse gas forcing to 1.5 Myr, past the change in climate dynamics known as the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, where glacial/interglacial cycles changed from a 40,000 to a 100,000 yr cyclicity. (CORDIS, Beyond EPICA, EU Horizon 2020 project 815384 (opens in a new tab))
  • Drilling progress to late 2024. By the end of this reporting period, on 30th November 2024 (M66), drilling activities had already reached a depth of 2059.74 meters. The drilling site is the Little Dome C camp (CORDIS, report of 23 May 2025 (opens in a new tab)). Later progress is not on that page and is not shown here.

Three proposed marine protected areas

  • Under consideration in 2023. Three more MPAs are currently being considered by CCAMLR in MPA planning domains 1 (Antarctic Peninsula), 3 (Weddell Sea) and 7 (East Antarctica). (CCAMLR, Spatial management, 31 March 2023 (opens in a new tab))
  • Status in 2026. A staged approach combining krill catch limits with parts of the Domain 1 proposal did not reach consensus: the Commission attempted to integrate revised catch limits, increased monitoring, and components of the proposed D1MPA in a staged manner to reduce the spatial concentration of the fishery. The Commission was unable to reach consensus on a revised conservation measure (CCAMLR-44, paragraph 4.56). (CCAMLR Fishery Report 2025 (opens in a new tab), section 1.1)

The outcome of CCAMLR’s October 2026 meeting (CCAMLR-45) had not been published when these sources were checked on 18 October 2026.

New specially protected areas

  • Two new areas in 2026. At the 48th Consultative Meeting in Hiroshima (11–21 May 2026): The ATCM established two new Antarctic Specially Protected Areas (ASPAs) and revised thirteen management plans. (Antarctic Treaty Secretariat, ATCM 48 – CEP 28 (opens in a new tab))
  • Where they are. Measure 14 covers Antarctic Specially Protected Area No 183 (Parts of Signy Island and adjacent islands, South Orkney Islands) and Measure 15 covers No 184 (Collins Bay, Kyiv Peninsula, Graham Coast, Antarctic Peninsula) (ATCM 48, measures table (opens in a new tab))
  • Emperor penguins. The Meeting expressed concern about risks to the emperor penguin and reaffirmed the importance of protecting the species as a priority. (ATCM 48 (opens in a new tab))
W

Weaknesses

Internal · harmful

Marine protection short of its target; krill catch limits not spread out

  • A network target for 2012. CCAMLR’s framework measure for marine protected areas recalls the aim in accordance with the decision at the World Summit on Sustainable Development (WSSD) in 2002 to achieve a representative network of MPAs by 2012, (CCAMLR Conservation Measure 91-04 (2011) (opens in a new tab), preamble)
  • Two protected areas in nine planning domains. From this work CCAMLR was able to identify 14 bioregions which were later grouped into 9 large-scale MPA planning domains. and The first high-seas MPA that was agreed by CCAMLR was the South Orkney Islands southern shelf MPA in 2009. The largest high-seas MPA in the world, the Ross Sea region MPA, was agreed in 2016 and came into force in 2017. (CCAMLR, Spatial management, 2023 (opens in a new tab)). The three further proposals are listed under Opportunities.
  • Spatial limits on krill lapsed in 2024. the Commission did not reach consensus to extend Conservation Measure 51-07 for one year for the fourth consecutive year, and it therefore expired at the end of the 2024 fishing season (CCAMLR-43, paragraph 4.45). (CCAMLR Fishery Report 2024 (opens in a new tab), section 1.1)
  • One overall limit since 2025. As such, starting in the 2025 fishing season, the total combined catch in Subareas 48.1–48.4 will be limited to 620,000 tonnes (Conservation Measure 51-01) without further spatial distribution of the trigger level. (CCAMLR Fishery Report 2024 (opens in a new tab), section 1.1). The 2025 catch is under Threats.

Liability annex not in force; the all-party approval rule

  • Adopted in 2005, still not in force. Annex VI to the Environmental Protocol, on liability arising from environmental emergencies, was Done: Stockholm June 17, 2005. Status: Entry into force: not yet in force (US Department of State, depositary status list, 6 October 2025 (opens in a new tab))
  • Every entitled party has to approve. The Annex becomes effective upon the date on which Measure 1 adopted at the XXVIIIth Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meeting has been approved by all Consultative Parties entitled to attend the XXVIIIth Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meeting. (depositary status list (opens in a new tab))
  • 19 of 28 approvals. Of the 28 Consultative Parties entitled to attend that 2005 meeting, 19 had approved by 6 October 2025. The nine that had not: Argentina, Belgium, Brazil, Bulgaria, China, India, Japan, the Republic of Korea and the United States. (Hawkeye’s count from the depositary list (opens in a new tab) and the Antarctic Treaty Secretariat list of Parties (opens in a new tab): the depositary lists 22 approvals, but Colombia and Türkiye are not Consultative Parties and Czechia became one only in 2014.)
  • The Treaty’s approval rule. Measures adopted at Consultative Meetings take effect only when every party entitled to take part has approved them: The measures referred to in paragraph 1 of this Article shall become effective when approved by all the Contracting Parties whose representatives were entitled to participate in the meetings held to consider those measures. (Antarctic Treaty (opens in a new tab), Article IX(4))

S

Strengths

Internal · helpful

Peaceful use only; no nuclear explosions

  • Military measures prohibited. Antarctica shall be used for peaceful purposes only. There shall be prohibited, inter alia, any measures of a military nature, such as the establishment of military bases and fortifications, the carrying out of military maneuvers, as well as the testing of any type of weapons. (Antarctic Treaty (opens in a new tab), Article I(1))
  • Military support for science is allowed. The present treaty shall not prevent the use of military personnel or equipment for scientific research or for any other peaceful purposes. (Antarctic Treaty (opens in a new tab), Article I(2))
  • No nuclear explosions or radioactive waste. Any nuclear explosions in Antarctica and the disposal there of radioactive waste material shall be prohibited. (Antarctic Treaty (opens in a new tab), Article V(1))

Mining banned except for science

  • The ban. Any activity relating to mineral resources, other than scientific research, shall be prohibited. (Environmental Protocol (opens in a new tab), Article 7)
  • A review is possible from 2048, not an expiry. It is sometimes publicly reported that the Protocol ‘expires’ in 2048. This is a misinterpretation and is not correct. Neither the Protocol, nor the Antarctic Treaty, have a termination date. From 2048, any of the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Parties can call for a review conference into the Protocol’s operation (Art. 25.2). (Antarctic Treaty Secretariat, The Protocol on Environmental Protection (opens in a new tab))
  • The ban stays unless a regime replaces it. Under Article 25(5) the prohibition shall continue unless there is in force a binding legal regime on Antarctic mineral resource activities (Environmental Protocol (opens in a new tab))

A natural reserve devoted to peace and science

  • The designation. The Parties commit themselves to the comprehensive protection of the Antarctic environment and dependent and associated ecosystems and hereby designate Antarctica as a natural reserve, devoted to peace and science. (Environmental Protocol (opens in a new tab), Article 2)
  • In force since 1998. The Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty was signed in Madrid on October 4, 1991 and entered into force in 1998. (Antarctic Treaty Secretariat (opens in a new tab))
  • 42 of 58 Treaty Parties have joined it. The Antarctic Treaty Secretariat’s list gives a Protocol date for all 29 Consultative Parties and 13 of the 29 Non-Consultative Parties (Hawkeye’s count from the list of Parties (opens in a new tab), 18 October 2026). The 16 that have not are listed on the GAP page.

Claims frozen and science shared

  • Territorial claims frozen. No acts or activities taking place while the present treaty is in force shall constitute a basis for asserting, supporting or denying a claim to territorial sovereignty in Antarctica or create any rights of sovereignty in Antarctica. No new claim, or enlargement of an existing claim, to territorial sovereignty in Antarctica shall be asserted while the present treaty is in force. (Antarctic Treaty (opens in a new tab), Article IV(2))
  • Results shared. The parties agree that, to the greatest extent feasible and practicable, scientific observations and results from Antarctica shall be exchanged and made freely available. (Antarctic Treaty (opens in a new tab), Article III(1)(c))
  • 58 Parties. Since 1959, 46 other countries have acceded to the Treaty. The Secretariat adds that there are now twenty-nine Consultative Parties in all. The other 29 Non-Consultative Parties are invited to attend the Consultative Meetings but do not participate in the decision-making. (Antarctic Treaty Secretariat, Parties (opens in a new tab))
  • 34 national programmes work together. COMNAP’s Members are the government National Antarctic Programs of 34 countries, and there are three observer programmes (COMNAP, Our Members (opens in a new tab)).
