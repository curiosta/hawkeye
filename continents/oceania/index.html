Oceania SWOT

Fourteen countries, nine territories and 46.6 million people, more than two-thirds of them in Australia and New Zealand. Four lenses, in the order the research started: Threats, Opportunities, Weaknesses, Strengths. Every figure was checked at its source on 19 October 2026.

T

Threats

External · harmful

A warming region

  • Second-warmest year on record. The State of the Climate in the South-West Pacific 2025 report documents how the region had its second warmest year on record (behind 2024), with extreme weather causing widespread disruption, economic damage, and loss of life. (WMO, 7 July 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • How much warmer. The 2025 annual mean surface air temperature for the region ranked as the second highest on record, at about 0.37 °C above the 1991–2020 average. (WMO, 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • Named the greatest threat by Forum Leaders. We reaffirm that climate change remains the single greatest threat to the livelihoods, security and wellbeing of the peoples of the Pacific and our commitment to progress the implementation of the Paris Agreement; (Boe Declaration on Regional Security (opens in a new tab), para 1(i), September 2018)

Scope: WMO’s South-West Pacific region also covers maritime South-East Asia (including Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines), not Oceania alone.

Also in PESTLE · Environmental

Rising seas and marine heatwaves

  • 3.7 mm a year. Over the period 1999–2025, sea level rose at an average rate of 3.7 ± 0.03 mm per year in the South-West Pacific region. (WMO, 7 July 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • Fastest around Australia and New Zealand. An elongated pattern of high rates of sea-level rise has been observed from the eastern coast of Australia to about 120°W longitude, encompassing the Coral and Tasman Seas and a large area west of New Zealand. (WMO, 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • Heatwaves across almost the whole ocean area. Marine heatwaves affected almost the entire ocean area of the South-West Pacific in 2025 (WMO, 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • More acidic water. Almost the entire South-West Pacific region experienced record low values for surface ocean pH in 2025. (WMO, 2026 (opens in a new tab))

Scope: WMO’s South-West Pacific region also covers maritime South-East Asia (including Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines), not Oceania alone. The region’s own response, the 2021 Declaration on Preserving Maritime Zones, is listed under Strengths.

Also in PESTLE · Environmental

Little climate finance reaching the region

  • About US$220 million a year. The Pacific region is currently receiving approximately US$220 million annually to address resilience and adaptation to climate change. This accounts for less than 0.22% of global climate finance. (Blue Pacific 2050 Dashboard, Climate change and disasters, Outcome 1.4 (opens in a new tab))
  • Measures still being agreed. For the same outcome the dashboard notes: Indicators are still under a validation process. (Blue Pacific 2050 Dashboard (opens in a new tab))

The dashboard gives no year or source for the US$220 million figure, so none is shown.

Also in PESTLE · Economic · GAP · Growth

Illegal and misreported tuna fishing

  • About 192,000 tonnes a year. The report estimated the total annual volume of tuna product harvested or transhipped in the Pacific involving IUU activity during 2017-19 to be 192,186 tonnes, worth some $USD333 million (ex-vessel value). (Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency, 13 December 2021 (opens in a new tab); IUU = illegal, unreported and unregulated)
  • Down from the previous study. This compares with a similar study conducted between 2010-15 where IUU was estimated at 306,440 tonnes, worth about $616 million. (FFA, 2021 (opens in a new tab))
  • Share of the catch. For context, the estimated IUU volume figure is around 6.5% of the total WCPFC Convention Area (WCPFC-CA) catch in 2019. (FFA working paper FFC120-WP.02 (opens in a new tab), para 3). The same paper finds that most of the volume comes from misreporting by licensed vessels, not from unlicensed fishing.

2017–19 are the latest years FFA has published.

O

Opportunities

External · helpful

A world court opinion on fixed maritime boundaries

  • No duty to redraw baselines. The Court considers that the provisions of UNCLOS do not require States parties, in the context of physical changes resulting from climate-change related sea level rise, to update their charts or lists of geographical co-ordinates that show the baselines and outer limit lines of their maritime zones once they have been duly established in conformity with the Convention. For this reason, States parties to UNCLOS are under no obligation to update such charts or lists of geographical co-ordinates. (International Court of Justice, Advisory Opinion of 23 July 2025 (opens in a new tab), para 362)
  • The region’s declaration was cited. The Court noted that many States had already expressed this view, citing among others the 2021 Pacific Islands Forum Declaration on Preserving Maritime Zones (ICJ, 2025 (opens in a new tab), para 360)

An advisory opinion states the Court’s view of the law; it is not a binding judgment.

Also in PESTLE · Legal

Partner money for the Pacific Resilience Facility

  • US$190 million pledged. With the latest commitments, total pledges to the PRF now stand at US$190 million, adding further momentum towards fully capitalising the Facility and strengthening its capacity to deliver practical support to communities across the Pacific. The facility has an initial capitalisation target of US$500 million (Pacific Islands Forum Secretariat, 9 October 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • New pledges from outside the region. The Government of Denmark also announced a US$2.3 million contribution, while the Government of the Netherlands pledged a further US$1 million (PIFS, 2026 (opens in a new tab)). A week later Ireland committed a further €2.65 million, bringing Ireland's total commitment to the Pacific Resilience Facility to €5.65 million to 2028. (Government of Ireland, 16 October 2026 (opens in a new tab))

The US$190 million total includes pledges by Pacific members themselves (for example Fiji, US$10 million) and predates Ireland’s October top-up. The facility itself is described under Strengths.

Tuna export markets

  • US$1.2 billion of exports. Tuna export value from FFA members reached US$1.2 billion in 2024, about 70% more than in 2015, and Exports to the four major markets surged ~70%. (FFA and SPC, Tuna Fishery Report Card 2025 (opens in a new tab))
  • Fees from foreign fleets. Government access & licence revenues at US$502m (+12% vs 2023), but broadly flat since 2017. (FFA and SPC, 2025 (opens in a new tab))

FFA’s 17 members include Australia and New Zealand.

W

Weaknesses

Internal · harmful

A large health gap with Australia and New Zealand

  • Premature deaths from chronic disease. The chance of dying between 30 and 70 from heart disease, cancer, diabetes or chronic respiratory disease was 30.9% in the Pacific islands in 2021 (32.9% in 2015), against 8.6% in Australia and New Zealand (UN SDG Global Database, series SH_DTH_NCOM (opens in a new tab), WHO estimates)
  • The highest country rates. In 2021, the point estimate ranged from 6.9% in the Republic of Korea to 44.1% in Kiribati. Estimates were also high in Solomon Islands (40.6%) and the Federated States of Micronesia (40.5%), indicating a concentration of very high premature mortality in several Pacific island countries. (WHO Western Pacific, Health Statistics 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • Child deaths. 36.2 deaths before age five per 1,000 live births in the Pacific islands in 2024 (50.3 in 2015), against 3.8 in Australia and New Zealand (UN SDG Global Database, series SH_DYN_MORT (opens in a new tab), UN IGME estimates)

“Pacific islands” here means the UN group Oceania excluding Australia and New Zealand (Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia), as published in the UN SDG Global Database.

Drinking water

  • About six in ten have a basic service. 62.6% of people in the Pacific islands used at least a basic drinking water service in 2024, up from 56.6% in 2015; in rural areas 53.6% (UN SDG Global Database, series SP_ACS_BSRVH2O (opens in a new tab), WHO/UNICEF JMP)
  • Oceania as a whole. 88.5% in 2024, lifted by Australia and New Zealand (UN SDG Global Database (opens in a new tab))

“Pacific islands” here means the UN group Oceania excluding Australia and New Zealand (Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia), as published in the UN SDG Global Database. The share with a safely managed service is not published for this group and is not shown.

Also in PESTLE · Environmental · GAP · Drinking water

Reading at the end of primary school

  • Half meet the expected level. A bit more than half (53%) of year six students are meeting the minimum expected reading performance levels, but a bit less than half are not (47%). (SPC, PILNA 2021 (opens in a new tab))
  • A small drop since 2018. Small decreases in reading performance were found in PILNA 2021 compared with PILNA 2018 at both year levels but it is unclear whether these are significant. (SPC, PILNA 2021 (opens in a new tab))

PILNA is the Pacific Islands Literacy and Numeracy Assessment, run across Pacific island countries.

Also in PESTLE · Social · GAP · Literacy

Little renewable generating capacity per person

  • 94 watts per person. Installed renewable electricity capacity in the Pacific islands was 93.6 watts per person in 2024, up from 65.4 in 2015 (UN SDG Global Database, series EG_EGY_RNEW (opens in a new tab), IRENA data)
  • Against the continent. Oceania as a whole had 1,526 watts per person in 2024, almost all of it in Australia and New Zealand (UN SDG Global Database (opens in a new tab))

“Pacific islands” here means the UN group Oceania excluding Australia and New Zealand (Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia), as published in the UN SDG Global Database.

S

Strengths

Internal · helpful

A nuclear-free zone

  • No nuclear explosive devices. Each Party undertakes: (a) Not to manufacture or otherwise acquire, possess or have control over any nuclear explosive device by any means anywhere inside or outside the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone; (b) Not to seek or receive any assistance in the manufacture or acquisition of any nuclear explosive device; (c) Not to take any action to assist or encourage the manufacture or acquisition of any nuclear explosive device by any State. (Treaty of Rarotonga (opens in a new tab), Art. 3; concluded 6 August 1985, in force 11 December 1986)
  • No warheads. No country in Oceania holds nuclear warheads (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 (opens in a new tab), January 2026)

Also in PESTLE · Political

A shared long-term plan and security framework

  • A 2050 vision agreed by Forum Leaders. As Pacific Leaders, our vision is for a resilient Pacific Region of peace, harmony, security, social inclusion and prosperity, that ensures all Pacific peoples can lead free, healthy and productive lives. (2050 Strategy for the Blue Pacific Continent (opens in a new tab), p. 6; endorsed in Suva in 2022)
  • Seven thematic areas. Political Leadership and Regionalism; People-Centered Development; Peace and Security; Resource and Economic Development; Climate Change and Disasters; Ocean and Environment; Technology and Connectivity (2050 Strategy (opens in a new tab), p. 12)
  • A wider idea of security. We affirm an expanded concept of security which addresses the wide range of security issues in the region, both traditional and non-traditional (Boe Declaration (opens in a new tab), para 1(vii), September 2018)

These are agreements, not measured outcomes. The Pacific Islands Forum has 18 members, including Australia and New Zealand.

Also in PESTLE · Political

A common position on maritime zones

  • Zones stay as they are. Forum members Proclaim that our maritime zones, as established and notified to the Secretary-General of the United Nations in accordance with the Convention, and the rights and entitlements that flow from them, shall continue to apply, without reduction, notwithstanding any physical changes connected to climate change-related sea-level rise. (Declaration on Preserving Maritime Zones (opens in a new tab), made 6 August 2021)

The International Court of Justice later referred to this declaration; see Opportunities.

Also in PESTLE · Legal

Island fleets take a growing share of the tuna catch

  • A record 61%. FFA Members' fleets took 61% of catch value in 2024 (up from 38% in 2015) — a record high. (FFA and SPC, Tuna Fishery Report Card 2025 (opens in a new tab))
  • More processing at home. Onshore processing nearly doubled since 2015, reaching ~286,000mt in 2024. (FFA and SPC, 2025 (opens in a new tab))

FFA’s 17 members include Australia and New Zealand.

Also in PESTLE · Economic · PESTLE · Legal

A Pacific-owned resilience fund

  • An international organisation since May 2026. The Agreement to Establish the Pacific Resilience Facility was signed in Honiara on 10 September 2025 and entered into force today following the joint presentation of instruments of ratification by Australia and Fiji in Suva (Pacific Islands Forum Secretariat, 6 May 2026 (opens in a new tab))
  • Based in Tonga. The headquarters of the PRF shall be established in Nuku'alofa, the Kingdom of Tonga. (Agreement to Establish the PRF (opens in a new tab), Art. 1(b))
  • What it funds. Climate adaptation, disaster preparedness, nature-based solutions, loss and damage projects and disaster rapid response through grants to its members (Agreement (opens in a new tab), Art. 3(a))
