United Kingdom TOWS

Pairs internal factors (Strengths, Weaknesses) with external ones (Opportunities, Threats) into four kinds of response.

Hawkeye analysis

Strategy cells on this page are Hawkeye’s own analysis. Each recommendation pairs a verified strength or weakness with a verified opportunity or threat, and compares it with a documented case from another place. The precedent facts are sourced. The pairing, the transfer and the recommendation are Hawkeye’s work, not official policy and not advice to any person or firm.

The documented responses listed lower on this page remain official programmes or instruments, each confirmed at their official source. Several of those responses already work in the same area as a recommendation. A recommendation is a pairing and a lesson from elsewhere. It is not a claim that United Kingdom has no policy in that area. Every figure links to its source and gives the year it refers to.

The four cells

  • SO Responses that use strengths to pursue opportunities

    • Use Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet to capture more world investment in clean energy

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Low-carbon power and Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet · SWOT · Opportunities · World investment in clean energy

      Use the UK’s low-carbon power base, including Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet, to attract more world investment in clean energy and in the supply chain around it. Official UK programmes already include the Energy Act, Great British Energy and the industrial strategy. This recommendation pairs that strength with the investment opportunity.

      Analogous case. The IEA reports: China has invested over USD 50 billion in new PV supply capacity – ten times more than Europe − and created as many as 300 000 manufacturing jobs across the solar PV value chain since 2011. Today, China’s share in all the manufacturing stages of solar panels (such as polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells and modules) exceeds 80%. Those policies contributed to a cost decline more than 80% (IEA, Special Report on Solar PV Global Supply Chains, revised August 2022 (opens in a new tab), Executive Summary).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. The UK already had a 64.3% low-carbon share of generation in 2025, just below the 2024 record, and hosts Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet (live SWOT). A documented manufacturing build-out elsewhere cut costs and created jobs. The IEA also warns about geographic concentration. The UK starts against an established low-carbon manufacturing centre, and a high generation share is not the same as a large domestic manufacturing share.

    • Use financial and professional services to grow exports under new trade agreements

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Financial services · SWOT · Opportunities · World demand for UK services, and new trade agreements

      Use the UK’s financial-services base to grow services exports under new trade agreements. Official UK trade and industrial programmes already exist. This recommendation pairs that strength with the services-trade opportunity.

      Analogous case. Estonia built a national digital identity and a secure public data exchange. The OECD writes that Estonia is a leader in e-government and has an innovative ICT sector (OECD Economic Surveys: Estonia 2024 (opens in a new tab), p. 16). The same survey says Estonia has prospered through participation in regional value chains, particularly in machinery and equipment, wood products and fast-growing exports of ICT services (p. 46).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. UK financial services are a live strength, and world demand for UK services plus new trade agreements is a live opportunity (live SWOT). Estonia shows that digital public systems and ICT-service exports can grow together in a trade-open economy. The analogy is limited because Estonia is a small EU member, and the OECD notes lagging digital use in traditional sectors and a large gender pay gap. Financial services are not the same as ICT services, and UK-EU trade barriers are a live threat on the same page.

  • ST Responses that use strengths to reduce threats

    • Cut imported-gas use by electrifying heat and road transport on low-carbon power

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Low-carbon power and Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet · SWOT · Threats · Dependence on imported gas

      Use the UK’s low-carbon power, including offshore wind, to electrify more heat and road transport, so that imported-gas dependence can fall. Official UK programmes already include the Climate Change Act, carbon budgets and the Energy Act. This recommendation pairs that power strength with the gas-import threat.

      Analogous case. The Norwegian Ministry of Transport, in “Norway is electric” (last updated 15 January 2025), states: At the end of 2024, electric vehicles (EVs) held an 88% market share in the market for new passenger cars in Norway, resulting in EVs making up 28% of the total passenger car stock. For new urban busses, the EV market share reached 72% by the end of 2024. The same page records a large fiscal cost for the incentives (Norwegian Ministry of Transport, 15 January 2025 (opens in a new tab)). Statistics Norway, 16 June 2025 (amended 11 November 2025), states: Energibruken i veitransport gikk ned 3,7 prosent i 2024 selv om de totale kjørelengdene for norsk-registrerte kjøretøy var omtrent uendret fra året før. Road-transport energy use fell 3.7% in 2024 even though total distances driven were about unchanged. Electric vehicles and assumed electric driving by plug-in hybrids stod for til sammen 29 prosent av de totale kjørelengdene i 2024, against 26% in 2023 and 2% in 2015 (Statistics Norway, 16 June 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. The UK already has a high low-carbon share of electricity, and imported gas is a live threat (live SWOT). Norway shows that high electric-vehicle uptake can go with a fall in road-energy use. The analogy is limited by Norway’s cleaner electricity mix and costly tax incentives, and by the fact that UK heat still uses large volumes of gas. Electricity generation share is not the same as the share of all energy. Official DESNZ figures also show the UK as a net electricity importer in 2024, so electrification will not automatically cut all import exposure.

  • WT Responses that reduce weaknesses and limit threats

    • Pay for verified habitat recovery as flood risk and wildlife loss continue

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Declining wildlife and waterbody health · SWOT · Threats · Flood risk as the climate warms (England)

      Pay landholders for independently verified habitat and waterbody recovery, so that wildlife and floodplain function can improve as flood risk rises in England. Official UK programmes already include the Environment Act species-abundance target. This recommendation pairs that wildlife weakness with the flood-risk threat.

      Analogous case. The World Bank records Costa Rica’s result: It is the first tropical country in the world to have reversed deforestation. Its highly biodiverse tropical rainforests now cover close to 60% of the country, having shrunk as low as 40% in 1987. Costa Rica also received a first Forest Carbon Partnership Facility payment of $16.4 million for independently verified emission reductions, for reducing 3.28 million tons of carbon emissions in 2018 and 2019 (World Bank, 16 November 2022 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. UK wildlife and waterbody health are declining, and flood risk in England is a live climate threat (live SWOT). Costa Rica shows that paying for verified forest results can go with a reversal of loss and a later results-based carbon payment. The analogy is limited because Costa Rica is a small tropical country, and the UK problem is temperate wildlife, rivers and floodplains, not a tropical deforestation reversal. A payment scheme does not by itself replace flood defences or land-use planning.

Documented national responses 11

Listed in no particular order. None is ranked.

  • Strategic Defence Review 2025 and Defence Investment Plan 2026

    Adopted by Ministry of Defence (review published 2 June 2025) and Ministry of Defence with HM Treasury (plan published 30 June 2026); both under the 2024–2026 government

    Defence Investment Plan: From 27-28 onwards, the UK will spend 2.7% of GDP on core NATO defence spending, solidifying its position as the NATO Alliance’s third-largest cash spender, behind only the US and Germany. Alongside NATO allies, the UK has committed to reach 3.5% of GDP on defence spending by 2035. It puts the total MOD budget for 2026–27 to 2029–30 at £297.7 billion (DIP funding explainer (opens in a new tab)). Earlier, the 2025 review’s introduction by the then Prime Minister said: We are delivering our commitment to spend 2.5% of GDP on defence, accelerating it to 2027, and we have set the ambition to reach 3% in the next Parliament, subject to economic and fiscal conditions. (Strategic Defence Review 2025 (opens in a new tab), PDF p.5).

    Related GAP · Defence spending 2.7% of GDP from 2027–28 and 3.5% by 2035 · SWOT · Threats · Russia’s war and threats to the UK · SWOT · Strengths · A continuous nuclear deterrent and large defence budget

  • Climate Change Act 2008: net zero by 2050

    Adopted by Parliament: Climate Change Act 2008 (c. 27), Royal Assent 26 November 2008; the 2050 target was raised to 100% by SI 2019/1056, in force since 27 June 2019

    s.1(1): It is the duty of the Secretary of State to ensure that the net UK carbon account for the year 2050 is at least 100% lower than the 1990 baseline. (Climate Change Act 2008, s.1 (opens in a new tab)).

  • Sixth and seventh carbon budgets

    Adopted by Secretary of State by order, each draft approved by both Houses: SI 2021/750 (in force 24 June 2021) and SI 2026/695 (in force 26 June 2026)

    The carbon budget for the 2033-2037 budgetary period is 965,000,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (Carbon Budget Order 2021 (opens in a new tab), art. 2). The carbon budget for the 2038-2042 budgetary period is 535,000,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (Carbon Budget Order 2026 (opens in a new tab), art. 2).

    Related GAP · Sixth and seventh carbon budgets (2033–2037 and 2038–2042)

  • Energy Act 2023

    Adopted by Parliament: Energy Act 2023 (c. 52), Royal Assent 26 October 2023; commenced in stages

    Scope, from the long title: An Act to make provision about energy production and security and the regulation of the energy market, including provision about the licensing of carbon dioxide transport and storage; about commercial arrangements for carbon capture and storage and for hydrogen production and transportation; about new technology, including low-carbon heat schemes and hydrogen grid trials; about the Independent System Operator and Planner; and other matters (Energy Act 2023 (opens in a new tab)). Commencement by part has not been checked (TBC).

  • Great British Energy

    Adopted by Parliament: Great British Energy Act 2025 (c. 16), Royal Assent 15 May 2025, in force the same day

    GOV.UK: Great British Energy will be a new, publicly owned, clean energy company. (GOV.UK, Great British Energy (opens in a new tab)). Its statutory objects, s.3(2), are restricted to facilitating, encouraging and participating in— (a) the production, distribution, storage and supply of clean energy, (b) the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions from energy produced from fossil fuels, (c) improvements in energy efficiency, (d) measures for ensuring the security of the supply of energy, and measures against slavery and human trafficking in its business (Great British Energy Act 2025, s.3 (opens in a new tab)).

    Related SWOT · Strengths · Low-carbon power and Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet

  • The UK’s Modern Industrial Strategy

    Adopted by Department for Business and Trade: CP 1451, published 23 June 2025 (after the Invest 2035 Green Paper of October 2024); published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government

    Our modern Industrial Strategy provides a new approach for a new era. It is robust, strategic, and unashamedly long-term: a 10-year plan to make the UK the best country to invest in anywhere in the world. (PDF p.8). It backs eight sectors: backing eight sectors (the IS-8) with the highest potential (Industrial Strategy, CP 1451 (opens in a new tab), PDF p.11). The Green Paper was a consultation that ran from 11 October to 25 November 2024 (GOV.UK, Invest 2035 (opens in a new tab)).

    Related GAP · Public R&D investment of £22.6 billion by 2029/30 (partial) · SWOT · Opportunities · Inward investment in artificial intelligence

  • National Wealth Fund

    Adopted by HM Treasury: the UK Infrastructure Bank has operated as the National Wealth Fund since 14 October 2024; published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government

    The National Wealth Fund will catalyse tens of billions of pounds of private investment into in the UK’s clean energy and growth industries, including green hydrogen, carbon capture and gigafactories. (the “into in” is in the source). The National Wealth Fund will have a total of £27.8 billion (HM Treasury, 14 October 2024 (opens in a new tab)). Whether legislation for a broader mandate has passed has not been checked (TBC).

  • Environment Act 2021: species abundance target

    Adopted by Parliament: Environment Act 2021 (c. 30), Royal Assent 9 November 2021; target set by SI 2023/91 (England)

    s.3(1): The Secretary of State must by regulations set a target (the “species abundance target”) in respect of a matter relating to the abundance of species. (Environment Act 2021, s.3 (opens in a new tab)). The regulations set it for England: The 2030 species abundance target is that the overall relative species abundance index on the specified date indicates that the decline in the abundance of species has been halted. (SI 2023/91, reg. 11(1) (opens in a new tab)).

How this TOWS matrix works

For a country, the responses are documented national laws, decrees, plans and programmes, each named with the body that adopted it.

Every documented national response is attributed to the body behind it and links to its official text. Those responses are not ranked. Strategy cells, where filled, are Hawkeye’s own analysis, based on analogous cases. They are not official policy and not advice.
