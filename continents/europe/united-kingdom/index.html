T

Threats

External · harmful

Russia’s war and threats to the UK

  • “An immediate and pressing threat.” The 2025 Strategic Defence Review calls Russia an immediate and pressing threat: Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine makes unequivocally clear its willingness to use force to achieve its goals, as well as its intent to re-establish spheres of influence in its near-abroad and disrupt the international order to the UK and its allies’ disadvantage. (Strategic Defence Review 2025 (opens in a new tab), Ministry of Defence, 2 June 2025, PDF p.31; published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government).
  • Beyond the battlefield. The same review says it will pose an enduring threat in key areas such as space, cyberspace, information operations, undersea warfare, and chemical and biological weapons. (Strategic Defence Review 2025 (opens in a new tab), PDF p.31).
  • Europe’s deadliest region in 2025. SIPRI reports: The security picture in Europe in 2025 was shaped primarily by the continuing Russia–Ukraine war, which meant that it was the region with the highest number of conflict-related fatalities. SIPRI estimates Russia’s military spending at US$190 billion in 2025, 7.5% of its GDP, and counts 5,420 Russian nuclear warheads in January 2026, against the United Kingdom’s 225 (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab); Russian spending is a SIPRI estimate).
  • The UK’s response. The United Kingdom spent US$89.0 billion on its military in 2025, 2.4% of GDP (SIPRI Fact Sheet, April 2026 (opens in a new tab)). The Defence Investment Plan of 30 June 2026 says: From 27-28 onwards, the UK will spend 2.7% of GDP on core NATO defence spending and the UK has committed to reach 3.5% of GDP on defence spending by 2035. (Defence Investment Plan funding explainer (opens in a new tab)). In his first speech as Prime Minister, Andy Burnham spoke of the need to honour our commitments on defence to our international partners. (GOV.UK, 20 July 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Dependence on imported gas

  • Gas is still the largest source of electricity. Gas remained the principal form of UK generation at 31.8 per cent, slightly outpacing wind’s contribution of 29.5 per cent of generation. (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab), 2025 data).
  • A net importer. The UK’s net import dependency stood at 43.3 per cent, down from 43.7 per cent in 2024. The bulk of the UK’s energy imports, over 90 per cent, comprise oil and gas and Norway is the UK’s primary supplier of energy imports. (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • North Sea decline. Production remains at the lowest level since 1973 as the natural decline of the mature North Sea basin continues. The UK has been a net importer of natural gas since 2004. (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab), ch. 4).
  • Where the gas comes from. Norway remained the UK’s largest import source, accounting for 69 per cent of total imports in 2025, down from 76 per cent in 2024, equivalent to 40 per cent of gross supply. Liquefied gas from the United States was the second source, at 23% of imports (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab), ch. 4).
  • Exposed to world prices. DESNZ records that Gas demand fell sharply in 2022, driven by high temperatures, high renewable output, and high energy and other prices following the Russia-Ukraine conflict. (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab), ch. 4).

Flood risk as the climate warms (England)

  • 6.3 million properties at risk. The new NaFRA shows that around 6.3 million properties (homes and businesses) in England are in areas at risk of flooding from one or a combination of sources: rivers, the sea and surface water (Environment Agency, NaFRA 2024 (opens in a new tab), §1.3; England only).
  • Rising with climate change. With climate change the total number of properties in areas at risk from rivers and the sea or surface water could increase to around 8 million by mid-century. In other words, 1 in 4 properties in England will be in areas at risk of flooding from rivers and the sea or surface water by mid-century. (Environment Agency, NaFRA 2024 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Better maps, not only more risk. The Environment Agency notes that the rise in properties at risk from surface water doesn’t reflect a real-world increase in risk; it mostly reflects better modelling (NaFRA 2024 (opens in a new tab), §1.3).
  • A threat driven by world emissions. Climate change depends on emissions worldwide; the United Kingdom produced 0.81% of world fossil CO2 in 2024 (see the table below). Flood-risk figures for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland come from separate assessments and have not been checked for this page (TBC).

Barriers to trade with the EU

  • A lasting cost in the official forecast. The Office for Budget Responsibility assumes that The post-Brexit trading relationship between the UK and EU, as set out in the ‘Trade and Cooperation Agreement’ (TCA) that came into effect on 1 January 2021, will reduce long-run productivity by 4 per cent relative to remaining in the EU. (OBR, Brexit analysis (opens in a new tab), last updated 20 July 2026).
  • Lower trade. Both exports and imports will be around 15 per cent lower in the long run than if the UK had remained in the EU. These are assumptions: This page sets out our assumptions related to EU exit that underpin our latest March 2026 forecast. (OBR (opens in a new tab)).
  • Why it is listed as a threat. Leaving the EU was a UK decision. This item covers the part outside UK control: the EU’s border and regulatory requirements on UK–EU trade under the TCA. The same OBR assumption appears in the PESTLE Legal factor.

Also in PESTLE · Legal

Cyber attacks by states and criminals

  • More nationally significant incidents. The National Cyber Security Centre received 1,727 incident tips in the year to 31 August 2025. These were triaged into 429 incidents requiring support from the NCSC IM team. However, nationally significant incidents represent 48% (204) of all incidents, a significant increase from last year (89). (NCSC Annual Review 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • The most serious cases are rising. Amongst this year's incidents, 4% (18) were categorised as highly significant in nature. This marks an almost 50% increase in highly significant incidents, an increase for the third consecutive year. (NCSC Annual Review 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • State actors. State actors continue to present a significant threat to UK and global cyber security, aided by an evolving cyber intrusion sector. Russia continues to act as a capable and irresponsible threat actor in cyberspace. The NCSC also names China, Iran and North Korea (NCSC Annual Review 2025, ch. 1 (opens in a new tab)).
  • An undercount. Reporting is not mandatory, so the NCSC says our data is not a true reflection of the number of cyber incidents that impact the UK (NCSC (opens in a new tab)). A 2026 review had not been published on 20 October 2026 (TBC).

Opportunities

External · helpful

World investment in clean energy

  • A record US$2.3 trillion in 2025. The Climate Change Committee reports: Global energy transition investment reached a record $2.3 trillion in 2025, exceeding investment in fossil fuels at an accelerating pace. (CCC, Progress report 2026 (opens in a new tab), 24 June 2026).
  • The UK’s offshore wind base. Offshore wind generation in 2025 was up by 6.6 per cent on 2024 to a new record of 52.0 TWh, despite reduced wind speed. Offshore wind continues to be the leading renewable technology in 2025 for generation, accounting for 60 per cent of all wind generation and 34 per cent of all renewable generation in 2025. (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab), ch. 6). The fleet’s size is under Strengths.
  • Plans to 2030. The Clean Power 2030 Action Plan (13 December 2024; published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government) says: We have high ambition. That means 43-50 GW of offshore wind, 27-29 GW of onshore wind (DESNZ, Clean Power 2030 Action Plan (opens in a new tab)). Its status under the present government has not been checked (TBC).

Also in PESTLE · Environmental

Inward investment in artificial intelligence

  • 51 inward AI projects in 2024. In 2024, there were 51 AI related inward investment projects into the UK, representing more than £15 billion in capital investment and expected to create more than 6,500 jobs. (DSIT, AI sector study 2024 (opens in a new tab), published 3 September 2025).
  • A growing sector to invest in. the UK’s AI ecosystem has grown to now include more than 5,800 AI companies – an 85% increase over the past 2 years. Revenue is now at £23.9 billion, and the sector contributed £11.8 billion in Gross Value Added (GVA). Investment in dedicated AI firms has rebounded to £2.9 billion in 2024 (DSIT (opens in a new tab); a study by Perspective Economics for DSIT).
  • A state AI institute. The AI Security Institute (formerly the AI Safety Institute) states: The AI Security Institute’s mission is to equip governments with a scientific understanding of the risks posed by advanced AI. AISI is part of the Cabinet Office. (GOV.UK, AI Security Institute (opens in a new tab)).

Also in PESTLE · Technological

European demand for defence equipment

  • £13.2 billion of export orders in 2024. The total value of UK export orders in 2024 was £13.2 billion, an increase of £1.3 billion in nominal prices compared to 2023. (MOD, UK Defence Export Statistics 2024 (opens in a new tab), 5 March 2026). The figures are orders, not deliveries, in nominal prices.
  • Driven by Europe. The main driver for the increase in 2024 was a £2.6 billion increase in the value of UK defence exports to Europe. Increases in export orders to Europe in 2024, were largely driven by major deals with Poland. (MOD (opens in a new tab)).
  • European spending at a record. SIPRI reports that military spending in Europe reached US$864 billion in 2025, up 14% on 2024 and the highest level SIPRI has recorded for the region (SIPRI Fact Sheet, April 2026 (opens in a new tab)). Statistics for 2025 UK export orders had not been published on 20 October 2026 (TBC).

World demand for UK services, and new trade agreements

  • £507 billion of services sold abroad in 2024. The trade in services surplus widened to 6.4% of GDP (£185.5 billion) in 2024, from 5.8% of GDP (£160.0 billion) in 2023. Exports of services increased from £460.9 billion to £507.0 billion, and imports increased from £301.0 billion to £321.5 billion. (ONS, Pink Book 2025 (opens in a new tab)). Financial services are under Strengths.
  • The latest quarter. the trade in services surplus increased by £2.5 billion to £51.3 billion, or 6.5% of GDP. Exports of services increased by £3.0 billion in Quarter 2 2026 to £137.3 billion. (ONS, Balance of payments, April to June 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • In the trans-Pacific partnership. The United Kingdom joined the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) on 15 December 2024; the agreement has been in force between the UK and all 11 other parties since Canada’s entry into force on 1 September 2026 (DBT, CPTPP collection (opens in a new tab), last updated 1 September 2026).
  • The official estimate of the gain. The agreement is expected to increase long-run UK GDP by around £2.0 billion each year when compared to projected levels of GDP in 2040 in the absence of the FTA. (DBT, CPTPP impact assessment (opens in a new tab), June 2024, p.34). At signing in July 2023 the government said: With the UK as a member, CPTPP will have a combined GDP of £12 trillion and account for 15% of global GDP. (DBT, 16 July 2023 (opens in a new tab)).

Weaknesses

Internal · harmful

Public debt, and productivity concentrated in London

  • Debt at 93.8% of GDP. Public sector net debt - the amount owed to the UK private sector and overseas, less liquid assets - was provisionally estimated at £2,985.5 billion at the end of August 2026, £78.5 billion more than a year earlier. Debt at the end of August 2026 was equivalent to 93.8% of GDP, 1.3 percentage points lower than a year earlier and at levels last seen in the early 1960s. (ONS, Public sector finances, August 2026 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Borrowing. The public sector borrowed £134.3 billion in the financial year ending March 2026, equivalent to 4.4% of GDP. (ONS (opens in a new tab)). The September 2026 figures had not been published on 20 October 2026.
  • London far ahead. in London, output per hour worked was 28.5% above that of the UK average (represented by 0.0%), and output per job was 35.3% above that of the UK average. (ONS, Regional and subregional labour productivity, 2023 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Most regions below the average. Output per hour worked levels for the South East were also above the UK average by 7.7%. All other regions showed output per hour worked levels as below the UK average. Wales, the West Midlands, the East Midlands and the North East had the four lowest levels of output per hour worked, at negative 15.1%, 14.8%, 14.7% and 14.6%, respectively compared with the UK average. (ONS (opens in a new tab)). A 2024 edition was not found (TBC).

Hospital waiting times (England)

  • 7.3 million waits. The number of RTT pathways where a patient was waiting to start treatment at the end of June 2026 was 7.3 million. Some patients are on multiple pathways. The number of unique patients is estimated to be around 6.2 million (NHS England, RTT statistical press notice, June 2026 data (opens in a new tab); RTT is referral to treatment).
  • Short of the 92% standard. In 65.8% of cases the patient had been waiting up to 18 weeks, thus not meeting the 92% standard. The NHS Constitution standard sets out that more than 92% of patients on incomplete pathways should have been waiting no more than 18 weeks from referral. (NHS England (opens in a new tab)). The standard comes from the NHS Constitution; it is not set in an Act.
  • England only; newer data pending a check. These figures cover England. Figures for August 2026 were published on 9 October 2026 but have not been read at source for this page (TBC). The target is tracked on the GAP page.

Child poverty

  • More than one child in four. In the financial year ending 2025, 27.4% of children (4.03 million) lived in relative low income after housing costs (DWP, Households below average income, FYE 1995–2025 (opens in a new tab), tables 1.4a and 1.4b). The release says: Relative low-income rates for children were 21% BHC and 27% AHC. (BHC and AHC: before and after housing costs).
  • About where it was in 2010/11. The rate was 27.2% in 2010/11 and 27.7% in the year ending 2024 (DWP, table 1.4a; DWP (opens in a new tab)).
  • A strategy with a modelled effect. The Child Poverty Strategy of 5 December 2025 (published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government) says: Taken together, action outlined in this strategy will lift 550,000 children out of relative low income after housing costs by the end of this parliament. (Cabinet Office, Our Children, Our Future (opens in a new tab)). This is a modelled reduction, not a rate target.

Declining wildlife and waterbody health

  • Priority species at 38% of their 1970 level. By 2023, the index of change in relative abundance of priority species in the UK had declined to 38% of its baseline value in 1970 (JNCC, UK Biodiversity Indicators 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • England’s species. Between 1970 and 2024 the index of change in relative abundance of species in England declined by around 40% (Defra, Indicators of species abundance in England (opens in a new tab), April 2026).
  • 14.3% of waterbodies in good health (England). The headline result is that 14.3% of waterbodies are classified as at Good Ecological Status. The Environment Agency adds that 2019’s 16.3% is not directly comparable because of method changes (Environment Agency, 7 August 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

Strengths

Internal · helpful

Financial services

  • A £74.7 billion surplus in 2024. UK financial services exports were £94.4 billion and imports £19.6 billion, a surplus of £74.7 billion (£69.8 billion in 2023). Insurance and pension services added a further £17.9 billion surplus (ONS, Pink Book 2025 (opens in a new tab), Table 3.1).
  • Part of a large services surplus. The total trade-in-services surplus was £185.5 billion in 2024, 6.4% of GDP (ONS, Pink Book 2025 (opens in a new tab)); world demand for UK services is under Opportunities.
  • Provisional figures. The ONS notes that financial-services figures since 2024 have been based on forecasts while its survey is redesigned. The Pink Book 2026 was not yet published on 20 October 2026.

Also in PESTLE · Economic

Education, human development and a free health service

  • Six in ten young adults have a degree-level qualification. The tertiary attainment rate among 25-34 year-olds increased from 52% in 2019 to 60% in 2024 and now exceeds the OECD average by 12 percentage points. (OECD, Education at a Glance 2025 (opens in a new tab)). With 2025 data the OECD gives 61.2%, 6th of 41 countries: The level of tertiary attainment among 25-34 year-olds is one of the highest among OECD and partner countries with available data. (OECD Education GPS (opens in a new tab)).
  • Second for international students. In absolute terms, the United Kingdom hosted almost 749 000 international students in 2023, which is the second highest number behind the United States with almost 957 000 international students. (OECD, Education at a Glance 2025 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Very high human development. The Human Development Index is 0.946, 13th of 193 countries and 11th of 42 in Europe (data year 2023; UNDP, HDR 2025 (opens in a new tab)). Life expectancy is lower in the European order, 24th of 43 (see the key facts).
  • A free health service in law (England). The Secretary of State must continue the promotion in England of a comprehensive health service designed to secure improvement in physical and mental health, and The services provided as part of the health service in England must be free of charge except in so far as the making and recovery of charges is expressly provided for by or under any enactment, whenever passed. (National Health Service Act 2006, s.1(1) and (4) (opens in a new tab)). Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own NHS laws (not checked, TBC). Waiting times are under Weaknesses.

A continuous nuclear deterrent and large defence budget

  • Always at sea. To ensure that the deterrent is not vulnerable to pre-emptive action by potential adversaries, the UK maintains four Ballistic Missile Submarines (SSBN, Ship Submersible Ballistic Nuclear) so that at least one will always be on a Continuous At Sea Deterrent patrol. (Strategic Defence Review 2025 (opens in a new tab), PDF p.101; published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government).
  • Replacement under way. Four Dreadnought class nuclear-powered ballistic submarines to replace the current Vanguard class. The first boat is due on patrol in the early 2030s. (Strategic Defence Review 2025 (opens in a new tab), PDF p.104).
  • 225 warheads. SIPRI counts 225 UK warheads in January 2026, 120 of them deployed (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab)). Russia’s arsenal is under Threats.
  • Sixth-largest military spender. The United Kingdom spent US$89.0 billion in 2025, 2.35% of GDP: 6th in the world and 3rd in Europe after Russia and Germany (SIPRI Military Expenditure Database (opens in a new tab); SIPRI Yearbook 2026 summary (opens in a new tab)).

Also in PESTLE · Political · GAP · Defence spending

Low-carbon power and Europe’s largest offshore wind fleet

  • Nearly two-thirds low-carbon. The share of generation coming from low carbon sources (nuclear plus renewables) fell slightly to 64.3 per cent in 2025, 0.4 percentage points lower than the record high share seen in 2024. (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 (opens in a new tab), ch. 5).
  • 16.6 GW of offshore wind. Installed offshore wind capacity was 16,570 MW at the end of 2025 (DESNZ, DUKES 2026 Table 6.2 (opens in a new tab)).
  • The largest in Europe. WindEurope counts 16,982 MW of grid-connected offshore wind in the United Kingdom at the end of 2025, 44% of Europe’s 38,617 MW; Germany is next with 9,624 MW (WindEurope, 2025 Statistics (opens in a new tab), February 2026, Table 1; share is Hawkeye’s arithmetic). WindEurope and DESNZ count capacity differently.
