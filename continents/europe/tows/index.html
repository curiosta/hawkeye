Europe TOWS

Pairs internal factors (Strengths, Weaknesses) with external ones (Opportunities, Threats) into four kinds of response.

Hawkeye analysis

Strategy cells on this page are Hawkeye’s own analysis. Each recommendation pairs a verified strength or weakness with a verified opportunity or threat, and compares it with a documented case from another place. The precedent facts are sourced. The pairing, the transfer and the recommendation are Hawkeye’s work, not official policy and not advice to any person or firm.

The documented responses listed lower on this page remain official programmes or instruments, each confirmed at their official source. Several of those responses already work in the same area as a recommendation. A recommendation is a pairing and a lesson from elsewhere. It is not a claim that Europe has no policy in that area. Every figure links to its source and gives the year it refers to.

The four cells

  • SO Responses that use strengths to pursue opportunities

    • Extend the power-sector cut into transport and buildings as renewables keep growing

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Emissions falling (EU-27) · SWOT · Opportunities · Renewable energy keeps growing

      Extend the emissions cuts already achieved in the power sector into transport and buildings, as renewable energy in Europe keeps growing. Official EU climate law and national plans already exist. This recommendation pairs the live emissions-falling strength with the renewable-energy opportunity, and uses the documented gap between sectors.

      Analogous case. The European Environment Agency states that in 2025, net EU greenhouse-gas emissions under the European Climate Law dropped by 39% below 1990 levels, while GDP grew by 74% and population by 8% over the same period. It also states: Since 2005, the annual rate of EU total greenhouse gas emission reductions more than doubled, reflecting stronger climate policy and the growing impact of the EU's Emissions Trading System (ETS). Emissions from EU ETS sectors have fallen substantially faster than those regulated primarily through national measures under the Effort Sharing Regulation framework. In 2025, year-on-year emissions rose in domestic transport (+0.3%) and buildings (+0.8%) (EEA, 9 November 2026 (opens in a new tab)). The 2025 figures are preliminary.

      Why it transfers, and the limit. EU-27 emissions are already falling, and renewable energy is still growing (live SWOT). The documented lesson is that the traded, power-heavy ETS sectors cut emissions faster than transport and buildings. The 2025 figures are preliminary, and those two end-use sectors saw emissions rise in 2025. Household investment and behaviour, which the EEA highlights, will limit the speed of the next stage. A 39% cut is also still short of the binding 55% net target for 2030.

  • ST Responses that use strengths to reduce threats

    • Cut imported fossil energy by continuing the transition that already reduced emissions

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Emissions falling (EU-27) · SWOT · Threats · Dependence on imported energy

      Continue the energy-system transition that already cut net EU emissions, so that dependence on imported fossil energy can fall. Official EU energy and climate measures already exist. This recommendation pairs falling emissions with the live import-dependence threat.

      Analogous case. The EEA records a 39% drop in net EU greenhouse-gas emissions below 1990 levels by 2025, with GDP up 74% over the same period, driven in part by transformation of the energy system (EEA, 9 November 2026 (opens in a new tab)). Eurostat, in a 18 March 2026 news article on 2024 data, states: The energy imports dependency rate in the EU was 57%, which means that nearly 60% of the EU’s energy needs were met by net imports. Oil and petroleum products accounted for 67% of energy imports into the EU in 2024, and natural gas for 24% (Eurostat, 18 March 2026 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Europe still depends on imported energy (live SWOT), even after a large, documented emissions cut. The transferable lesson is that power-sector change can cut emissions while output grows. The limit is that the opened Eurostat article gives the 2024 import-dependency rate, not a verified multi-year decline, and oil remains the largest import category. A 57% dependency rate shows that emissions cuts have not yet removed the import threat. Progress also varies widely among member states, from 5% in Estonia to 98% in Malta in the same article.

  • WT Responses that reduce weaknesses and limit threats

    • Pay for forest recovery so Europe’s carbon sink can strengthen as the continent warms

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Forests absorb less carbon (EU-27) · SWOT · Threats · The fastest-warming continent

      Pay landholders for verified forest protection and restoration, so that the EU forest carbon sink can strengthen as the continent warms. Official EU climate and forestry measures already exist. This recommendation pairs the live forest-sink weakness with the fastest-warming-continent threat.

      Analogous case. The World Bank records that Costa Rica is the first tropical country in the world to have reversed deforestation, with rainforests now covering close to 60% of the country, having shrunk as low as 40% in 1987, and that it received US$16.4 million for 3.28 million tons of independently verified emission reductions in 2018 and 2019 (World Bank, 16 November 2022 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. EU-27 forests absorb less carbon than before, and Europe is the fastest-warming continent (live SWOT). Paying for verified results can change land-use incentives, as in Costa Rica. The analogy is limited because Costa Rica is a small tropical country, and the EU sink problem is also about climate stress, pests and harvest, not only historic clearance. A tropical payment scheme does not transfer unchanged to temperate production forests.

Listed in no particular order. None is ranked.

  • European Climate Law, as amended for 2040

    Adopted by European Parliament and Council (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), Regulation (EU) 2021/1119, in force 29 July 2021, amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/667, in force 7 April 2026

    Quoted on the GAP page: net zero by 2050, at least 55% less by 2030 and 90% less by 2040, against 1990.

    Related SWOT · Strengths · Emissions falling (EU-27) · GAP · EU climate neutrality, 2050 · GAP · EU net greenhouse gas emissions, 2040

  • Strategic Agenda 2024–2029

    Adopted by European Council (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), adopted 27 June 2024, a political agenda, not a law

    We will strengthen our competitiveness and become the first climate-neutral continent, making a success of the climate and digital transitions, leaving no one behind. (European Council, Strategic Agenda 2024–2029 (opens in a new tab)) Going forward, we will invest substantially more and better together, reduce our strategic dependencies, scale up our capacities and strengthen the European defence technological and industrial base accordingly. (European Council, Strategic Agenda 2024–2029 (opens in a new tab))

  • Net-Zero Industry Act

    Adopted by European Parliament and Council (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), Regulation (EU) 2024/1735, in force 29 June 2024

    The general objective of this Regulation is to improve the functioning of the internal market by establishing a framework in order to ensure the Union’s access to a secure and sustainable supply of net-zero technologies, (Article 1(1)). Its benchmark is a benchmark of at least 40 % of the Union’s annual deployment needs for the corresponding technologies necessary to achieve the Union’s 2030 climate and energy targets; (Article 5(1)(a)) (Net-Zero Industry Act (opens in a new tab)). It is a benchmark, not a binding quota.

    Related SWOT · Opportunities · Renewable energy keeps growing

  • Critical Raw Materials Act

    Adopted by European Parliament and Council (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), Regulation (EU) 2024/1252, in force 23 May 2024

    The general objective of this Regulation is to improve the functioning of the internal market by establishing a framework to ensure the Union’s access to a secure, resilient and sustainable supply of critical raw materials, including by fostering efficiency and circularity throughout the value chain. (Critical Raw Materials Act (opens in a new tab), Article 1(1)). Its 2030 figures for extraction, processing and recycling are benchmarks, not binding targets.

    Related SWOT · Threats · Dependence on imported energy

  • Security Action for Europe (SAFE)

    Adopted by Council of the EU (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), Regulation (EU) 2025/1106, in force 29 May 2025

    This Regulation establishes the Security Action For Europe (SAFE) through the Reinforcement of the European Defence Industry Instrument (the ‘SAFE instrument’) providing financial assistance to Member States, enabling them to carry out urgent and major public investments in support of the European defence industry in response to the current crisis situation. The maximum amount of financial assistance in the form of loans provided under the SAFE instrument shall be EUR 150 000 000 000. (SAFE Regulation (opens in a new tab))

    Related SWOT · Threats · War and rearmament

  • European Defence Industry Programme (EDIP)

    Adopted by European Parliament and Council (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), Regulation (EU) 2025/2643, in force 30 December 2025

    Article 3 sets the programme’s budget at EUR 1 200 000 000 in current prices, and the Ukraine Support Instrument for 30 December 2025 to 31 December 2027 at EUR 300 000 000 in current prices (EDIP Regulation (opens in a new tab)).

    Related SWOT · Threats · War and rearmament

  • Artificial Intelligence Act

    Adopted by European Parliament and Council (EU-27, 60.1% of Europe’s population), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

    Quoted in the SWOT Opportunities (EU AI Act (opens in a new tab), Article 1(1)).

    Related SWOT · Opportunities · A single rulebook for AI

  • Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence (CETS No. 225)

    Adopted by Council of Europe, 46 member states (41 in Europe, 79.2% of its population), and other signatories, opened for signature 5 September 2024, binds only its parties. Whether it is in force remains TBC

    The provisions of this Convention aim to ensure that activities within the lifecycle of artificial intelligence systems are fully consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law. (CETS No. 225 (opens in a new tab), Article 1(1)). This Convention shall enter into force on the first day of the month following the expiration of a period of three months after the date on which five signatories, including at least three member States of the Council of Europe, have expressed their consent to be bound by this Convention in accordance with paragraph 2. (CETS No. 225 (opens in a new tab), Article 30(3)). The Treaty Office recorded a ratification by Bosnia and Herzegovina on 28 October 2026 (Council of Europe Treaty Office, October 2026 (opens in a new tab)). An official current party list was not opened for this page, so whether the convention is in force remains TBC.

How this TOWS matrix works

For a continent, the responses are documented instruments of the regional and treaty bodies its countries belong to, each named with its members and the date it was adopted.

Every documented regional response is attributed to the body behind it and links to its official text. Those responses are not ranked. Strategy cells, where filled, are Hawkeye’s own analysis, based on analogous cases. They are not official policy and not advice.
