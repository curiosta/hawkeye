Hawkeye analysis

Strategy cells on this page are Hawkeye’s own analysis. Each recommendation pairs a verified strength or weakness with a verified opportunity or threat, and compares it with a documented case from another place. The precedent facts are sourced. The pairing, the transfer and the recommendation are Hawkeye’s work, not official policy and not advice to any person or firm.

The documented responses listed lower on this page remain official programmes or instruments, each confirmed at their official source. Several of those responses already work in the same area as a recommendation. A recommendation is a pairing and a lesson from elsewhere. It is not a claim that Asia has no policy in that area. Every figure links to its source and gives the year it refers to.

The four cells

  • SO Responses that use strengths to pursue opportunities

    • Build a deeper solar chain on Asia’s industrial base and South-East Asian renewable plans

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Progress on industry and infrastructure · SWOT · Opportunities · Renewable power in South-East Asia

      Use Asia’s industrial and infrastructure base to build more of the solar supply chain inside the region, in line with South-East Asian renewable-power plans. Official national and regional energy programmes already exist. This recommendation pairs that industrial strength with the renewable-power opportunity.

      Analogous case. The IEA reports: China has invested over USD 50 billion in new PV supply capacity – ten times more than Europe − and created as many as 300 000 manufacturing jobs across the solar PV value chain since 2011. Today, China’s share in all the manufacturing stages of solar panels (such as polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells and modules) exceeds 80%. Those policies contributed to a cost decline more than 80% (IEA, Special Report on Solar PV Global Supply Chains, revised August 2022 (opens in a new tab), Executive Summary).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Asia already has a large industrial base, and renewable power in South-East Asia is a live opportunity (live SWOT). A documented build-out inside the region already cut costs and created manufacturing jobs. The IEA also warns that high geographical concentration creates challenges. A deeper chain in other Asian countries starts against an established low-cost centre, and jobs and investment will not automatically spread evenly across the region.

  • ST Responses that use strengths to reduce threats

    • Cut Asia’s fossil carbon with policy mixes that already reduced emissions while output grew

      Pairs SWOT · Strengths · Progress on industry and infrastructure · SWOT · Threats · Fast warming and glacier loss

      Apply policy mixes that already cut net greenhouse-gas emissions while economies grew, so that Asia’s large fossil-carbon share can fall as the region warms and glaciers are lost. Official national climate pledges already exist. This recommendation is a lesson from a measured regional cut.

      Analogous case. The European Environment Agency states: In 2025, net EU greenhouse gas emissions under the scope of the European Climate Law dropped by 39% below 1990 levels, while GDP and population grew by 74% and 8% respectively over the same period (EEA, 9 November 2026 (opens in a new tab)). The 2025 figures are preliminary, the pace of cuts slowed in 2025, and emissions rose that year in domestic transport and buildings.

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Asia is the largest source of fossil CO2 and is warming fast, with glacier loss (live SWOT). The EU case shows that emissions and economic output can move in opposite directions over decades. The figures are preliminary for 2025. The EU started with a different energy mix, a carbon market and slower demand growth than many Asian economies. Transport and buildings remain hard, even in the EU.

  • WO Responses that use opportunities to reduce weaknesses

    • Use trade and targeted public action to cut hunger in Asia

      Pairs SWOT · Weaknesses · Hunger · SWOT · Opportunities · The RCEP free-trade partnership

      Use the RCEP trade partnership, together with targeted public action, to cut hunger, rather than relying on growth alone. Official national food programmes and RCEP already exist. This recommendation pairs Asia’s live hunger weakness with that trade opportunity, and uses a documented poverty reduction inside the region.

      Analogous case. A joint World Bank study with Chinese institutions states: Over the past 40 years, the number of people in China with incomes below $1.90 per day – the International Poverty Line as defined by the World Bank to track global extreme poverty– has fallen by close to 800 million. With this, China has contributed close to three-quarters of the global reduction in the number of people living in extreme poverty. At China’s national poverty line, the number of poor fell by 770 million over the same period. The report points to two pillars: broad-based economic transformation, and targeted support to places and later to households (World Bank, 1 April 2022 (opens in a new tab)).

      Why it transfers, and the limit. Hunger remains a live weakness in Asia, and RCEP is a live trade opportunity (live SWOT). The Chinese case shows a very large, documented fall in extreme poverty, with targeted support as well as an outward-oriented transformation. The $1.90 line is not the same as every national food-security measure, and the same report notes remaining rural-urban gaps. China’s starting conditions and institutions do not transfer as a package, and a regional trade pact does not by itself deliver household-level nutrition.

Documented regional responses 8

Listed in no particular order. None is ranked.

  • Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP)

    Adopted by 15 parties (13 in Asia); signed 15 November 2020, in force 1 January 2022

    A free-trade and investment partnership (RCEP Agreement (opens in a new tab), Art. 1.3). Objective quoted on the SWOT page.

    Related SWOT · Opportunities · The RCEP free-trade partnership

  • Bangkok Treaty (South-East Asia Nuclear Weapon-Free Zone)

    Adopted by Ten ASEAN states parties; signed 15 December 1995, in force 27 March 1997

    Parties renounce nuclear weapons inside and outside the zone (Bangkok Treaty (opens in a new tab), Art. 3(1)). Text quoted on the SWOT page.

    Related SWOT · Strengths · A South-East Asian nuclear-weapon-free zone

  • ASEAN Agreement on Disaster Management and Emergency Response (AADMER)

    Adopted by ASEAN members; signed 26 July 2005, in force 24 December 2009

    The objective of this Agreement is to provide effective mechanisms to achieve substantial reduction of disaster losses in lives and in the social, economic and environmental assets of the Parties, and to jointly respond to disaster emergencies through concerted national efforts and intensified regional and international co-operation. (AADMER (opens in a new tab), Art. 2)

    Related SWOT · Threats · Floods, heat and drought in 2025

  • Charter of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation

    Adopted by 10 members, 8 in Asia; signed 7 June 2002, in force 19 September 2003

    The main SCO goals and tasks shall be: strengthening mutual trust, friendship and goodneighborliness between the member States (SCO Charter (opens in a new tab), Art. 1)

    Related SWOT · Threats · Rising military spending and five nuclear-armed states

  • Charter of the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC)

    Adopted by 8 South Asian members; Dhaka, 8 December 1985

    The objectives of the ASSOCIATION shall be: a) to promote the welfare of the peoples of SOUTH ASIA and to improve their quality of life; (SAARC Secretariat, SAARC Charter (opens in a new tab), Art. I)

  • Charter of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)

    Adopted by 6 Gulf states; 25 May 1981

    The basic objectives of the Cooperation Council are: 1. To effect coordination, integration and inter-connection between Member States in all fields in order to achieve unity between them. (GCC Charter (opens in a new tab), Art. 4)

  • Intergovernmental Agreement on the Asian Highway Network

    Adopted by Adopted in Bangkok, 18 November 2003; in force 4 July 2005

    Its preamble begins: Conscious of the need to promote and develop international road transport in Asia and with neighbouring regions, (UNTS Vol. 2323, No. 41607 (opens in a new tab))

    Related SWOT · Opportunities · Mobile networks and road links

  • ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation (APAEC) Phase II

    Adopted by ASEAN energy ministers, 19 November 2020; 2021–2025, completed; succeeded by APAEC 2026–2030

    Sub-theme: Accelerating Energy Transition and Strengthening Energy Resilience through Greater Innovation and Cooperation (APAEC Phase II (opens in a new tab)). Its 2025 targets are on the GAP page.

    Related SWOT · Opportunities · Renewable power in South-East Asia

How this TOWS matrix works

For a continent, the responses are documented instruments of the regional and treaty bodies its countries belong to, each named with its members and the date it was adopted.

Every documented regional response is attributed to the body behind it and links to its official text. Those responses are not ranked. Strategy cells, where filled, are Hawkeye’s own analysis, based on analogous cases. They are not official policy and not advice.
